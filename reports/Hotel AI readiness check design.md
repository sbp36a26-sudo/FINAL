# Close the loopholes before adding a score

The readiness check the team plans is a good idea that no one else has built: none of the ten well-known AI or digital readiness tools found scores readiness **per use case**, and no hospitality body offers a scored AI self-check, so a short per-use-case check for hotels fills a real gap. The six must-have gates sit on the factors that adoption research and European hotel surveys rate most important (management backing, an owner, money, system fit, a measured baseline, lawful data use), so the core of the D6 framework holds. The problem is the scoring, not the idea. Re-running the tool's own code on profiles the researcher built (not real hotel answers) shows that a hotel can reach **"Ready to scale" with all six gates answered N/A**, or with only the gates answered, and that leaving doubtful questions blank lifts a hotel from "pilot" to "scale". These loopholes must be closed before any 0–100 number goes on screen, because research on self-assessment consistently finds organisations overrate themselves. Once they are closed, the recommended overall score is a **"banded mean"**: the average of the six area percentages, capped at 39 when the verdict is "Build foundations first" and at 74 when it is "Ready to pilot", so the number can never contradict the verdict. The gates should become **proportional** (lighter budget and no integration test for light subscription tools) and **conditional** (staff-representative consultation, DPIA and human override only when the tool touches staff, sensitive data or acts on its own). Three things the check does not ask today, and that the evidence says matter, should be added without lengthening it much: whether staff believe in the tool, how guests will react, and whether the vendor will support integration and let the hotel take its data away. Plan v1 survives largely intact; the changes are concentrated in its answer options, its gate list and its score formula.

Most web sources could not be opened in full during the research; those findings come from search-engine summaries or abstracts and carry the mark **(secondary)**: check the original before quoting them in the final report. Facts read directly from the team's own files (the readiness tool at `tools/readiness/index.html` and its evidence IDs from the D6 evidence pack, master v8_3) are marked **(team file)**. The researchers' own reasoning is marked **(inference)**. All stress-test figures come from re-running the tool's own scoring code on profiles the researcher constructed, not on real hotel answers.*

## No public tool scores readiness per use case, and none proves people finish it

The established tools share one template. They assess the **whole organisation** on **five to seven dimensions** (strategy, data, infrastructure, governance, skills, culture), turn answers into a 0–100 or 1–5 score, and place the organisation in **four or five named stages** with a profile per dimension. The EU's EDIH Digital Maturity Assessment, built by the Joint Research Centre for SMEs and public bodies, rates each question 0–10 and each of six dimensions 0–100, gives an overall and a per-dimension score, and is designed to be **repeated** before and after an intervention, three times per substantial service (secondary) ([EDIH DMAT overview](https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/knowledge-hub/guidance-documents/overview-digital-maturity-assessment-tool-dmat); [DMA FAQ](https://european-digital-innovation-hubs.ec.europa.eu/faq/dma-frequently-asked-questions-faq)). Cisco's AI Readiness Index uses six pillars and **49 indicators**, weighted Infrastructure 25%, Data 20%, Strategy, Governance and Talent 15% each, Culture 10%, and sorts firms into four groups from "Pacesetters" to "Laggards"; its 2025 edition surveyed **8,000+ senior IT and business leaders in 30 markets and 26 industries** (secondary) ([Cisco methodology](https://www.cisco.com/c/m/en_us/solutions/ai/readiness-index/methodology.html); [RCR Wireless, Dec 2025](https://www.rcrwireless.com/20251209/ai/cisco-ai-readiness-index-2025)). Microsoft's free assessment has **45 multiple-choice questions taking about 45 minutes** across seven pillars, with five stages from "exploring" to "realizing" (secondary, blog) ([Augment Code review](https://www.augmentcode.com/tools/ai-readiness-assessment-tools)). Gartner's seven-pillar toolkit is mostly for its clients, and Deloitte, McKinsey and PwC models are consulting-led rather than free self-checks (secondary) ([Gartner toolkit](https://www.gartner.com/en/chief-information-officer/research/ai-maturity-model-toolkit); [Augment Code](https://www.augmentcode.com/tools/ai-readiness-assessment-tools)).

The closest precedent for a per-use-case unit is not an AI maturity model at all but the UK government's **dataset-level** checklist ("is this dataset AI-ready?"), which returns a ready/not-ready answer and a fix list (secondary) ([ai.gov.uk: Making data ready for AI](https://ai.gov.uk/knowledge-hub/how-to/Making-data-ready-for-AI/)). Explicit gates or minimum requirements are rare in maturity tools (inference from the ten tools compared). That makes the team's design, one use case, a few must-haves and a go/no-go verdict, a point of difference rather than a copy of an existing format (inference).

### Uptake follows a service, and no tool publishes completion rates

Usage figures exist only for public tools tied to a service. By September 2024 the EDIH tool had collected **almost 9,000 SME assessments and about 800 public-sector assessments** (base: EDIH customers across the EU network; the same JRC report gives 9,208 elsewhere) (secondary) ([JRC, Carpentier et al., EUR 40272](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/bitstream/JRC140547/JRC140547_01.pdf)). Austria's KMU.DIGITAL, which opens with a funded status analysis, reports **over 25,000** funded consulting and implementation projects on a budget of about EUR 25 million (base: WKO programme materials, 2025) (secondary) ([WKO kmu.digital 2025](https://wko.at/oe/information-consulting/kmu.digital-2025.pdf)). In both cases the assessment is the entry ticket to help, not a standalone quiz (inference). **No source reported completion or drop-off rates for any online readiness tool**, and no study links using such a tool to better AI project outcomes; MIT CISR links a firm's maturity *stage* to financial performance (base: survey of 721 companies, Oct 2022, mainly pre-GenAI AI), which is a correlation about firms, not evidence about tools (secondary) ([MIT CISR briefing](https://cisr.mit.edu/publication/2024_1201_EnterpriseAIMaturityModel_WeillWoernerSebastian)). For the team this means the claim "about 5 minutes" and any claim that the check will be "used" are design targets to test, not evidence-based figures.

### Self-assessments run optimistic, so questions must ask for facts

Maturity models have been criticised since the mid-2000s for weak testing of validity and for undocumented construction (de Bruin & Rosemann 2005; Becker, Knackstedt & Pöppelbuß 2009) (secondary) ([Becker et al., author version](https://webdocs.uni.li/public/04046167.PDF)). Studies comparing self-ratings with outside judgement find systematic **overestimation**. In a Swiss digital-transformation survey, **60% of establishments rated their technology above average**, which the authors call mathematically impossible (secondary) ([arXiv 2412.12784](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.12784)). A Forrester survey found **65% of procurement leaders saw themselves as "advanced"** while Forrester judged fewer than 12% to be there (secondary, trade press) ([SupplyChainBrain](https://www.supplychainbrain.com/blogs/1-think-tank/post/29842-companies-are-wildly-overestimating-their-digital-maturity-in-supplier-management)). A survey of **2,517 managers in 120 organisations in nine countries** found about 75% of management teams rated their organisation above average (secondary, abstract only) ([Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/14635771111166848/full/html)), and an expert check of SME cybersecurity found self-reports consistently rosier than observation (secondary) ([Beyond Self-Reporting](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399959349_Beyond_Self-Reporting_Uncovering_the_Operational_Realities_of_SME_Cybersecurity_Through_Expert_Assessment)). None of these studies is about hotels or AI. The practical lesson (inference) is that questions should ask about **observable facts** ("Do you have a number and a date for this KPI?") rather than self-ratings ("How mature is your data?"), and that the must-haves should ask for a short **proof note**.

### Hospitality has research surveys but no scored self-check

No public, scored AI-readiness self-assessment from HFTP, HOTREC, AHLA/HTNG or a national tourism body was found (one combined search; secondary). The nearest item is a research survey: Britz & Schegg (HES-SO Valais, 2025) sent a 13-question AI-readiness questionnaire to **408 hoteliers**, with about **79–80 responses, 71% of them Swiss**, July–September 2025 (team file: ASN-002; secondary) ([Britz & Schegg 2025](https://www.hotellerie.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Dokumente/Studien_und_Umfragen/Britz_Schegg_2025_AI_Readiness_in_Hotels.pdf)). A vendor "Hotel AI Readiness Score" exists but is marketing content and should not be cited (secondary) ([pulse.bot](https://www.pulse.bot/hotels/news/what-is-a-hotel-ai-readiness-score-and-why-every-gm-should-know-theirs-d16c936c-e699-486d-a938-1b3b7cd47d19/)). The HES-SO surveys are the only honest basis for a later peer comparison, so leaving the benchmark out of v1, as the plan does, is right (inference).

## The six gates rest on the strongest adoption factors, but the check measures ability, not willingness

Adoption research has two families. Firm-level models, chiefly TOE (technology, organisation, environment; Tornatzky & Fleischer 1990) and Rogers' diffusion of innovations, dominate empirical firm-level studies (Oliveira & Martins 2011) (secondary) ([IHMT record](https://www.ihmt.unl.pt/publications/3741/)). They stress technology fit, management support, resources, skills, data and outside pressure from competitors, customers, regulators and vendors. Individual-level models (TAM; UTAUT, which explained about 69% of variance in usage intention in its original test) add whether the people who must use a tool expect it to help and find it easy (secondary) ([Venkatesh et al. 2003](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3375136)). Weiner's theory of organisational readiness adds a sharper point: readiness is staff **commitment** (they value this change) plus **efficacy** (they believe they can deliver it), and resources feed efficacy but are not readiness itself (secondary) ([Weiner 2009, Implementation Science](https://implementationscience.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1748-5908-4-67)). The AI-specific framework of Jöhnk, Weißert & Wyrtki (2021), built from 25 expert interviews, groups **18 factors into five categories: strategic alignment, resources, knowledge, culture and data** (secondary) ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/binfse/v63y2021i1d10.1007_s12599-020-00676-7.html)); Mikalef & Gupta (2021) split AI capability into tangible, human (technical and managerial skills) and intangible resources such as coordination across departments (secondary) ([ESCP summary](https://escp.eu/thechoice/tl-dr/four-non-tech-conditions-for-ai-to-improve-performance/)).

In AI and hospitality studies, **top-management support and competitive pressure** come out significant most often. An IJCHM study of hospitality AI adoption found both among the strongest effects, alongside perceived risk and regulatory support (secondary, abstract) ([IJCHM-04-2022-0433](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/IJCHM-04-2022-0433)). AL-Khatib (2024), on generative AI in Jordanian hospitality, found competitive pressure, regulatory support and **vendor support** significant, complexity negative and **cost not significant** (secondary, abstract) ([Management Decision](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/MD-09-2023-1525/full/html)). A study of **1,600 employees of independent US hotels** found employee readiness and acceptance significantly affect AI adoption (secondary) ([Jerez-Jerez 2024, IJHM](https://researchportal.beds.ac.uk/en/publications/a-study-of-employee-attitudes-towards-ai-its-effect-on-sustainabl-4/)). None of these samples is European, and effect sizes were not visible.

The mapping against the tool (team file: 31 questions in six areas; gates P1, F1, F2, T1, D5, O1) shows a clean fit at the core. O1 (approved plan) covers management support and strategic alignment; P1 (named owner) the champion; F1 (budget) resources; T1 (two-way data exchange) compatibility; F2 (KPI baseline) measurable advantage; D5 (lawful basis and vendor agreement) regulation. That is a defensible, literature-backed set of must-haves (inference). The gaps are elsewhere:

| Factor the research finds important | Coverage in today's tool (team file) | What to do (inference) |
|---|---|---|
| Staff believe the tool helps them (Weiner commitment; UTAUT; Jerez-Jerez) | Missing. O6 asks if staff feel able to question AI, and it is flagged for only 4 of 91 use cases | Add one staff-belief question in People, non-gate |
| Guest acceptance and a route to a human (TOE customer pressure) | Missing | Add one question for guest-facing tools, non-gate |
| Vendor support, integration help, data exit (AL-Khatib; TOE external support) | Partly (O4 tests claims, T5 fallback, G4 security) | Widen O4 rather than add a question |
| Technical skills, in-house or from the vendor (Mikalef & Gupta) | Building block "Specialist skills" has **no question attached** (`codes: []`) | Extend P3 to cover set-up and maintenance skills |
| Coordination between IT, operations and the department | Partly (O3 only for managed/franchised) | Reword O1 to name who agrees |
| Competitive, brand or owner pressure (TOE environment) | Missing | Ask once, show as unscored context; do not score it |

The team's own evidence already shows why the belief gap matters: Marriott's first room-assignment pilot failed because staff overrode every assignment (team file: SKF-042-02) ([Hospitality.today](https://www.hospitality.today/article/how-marriott-is-using-ai-to-rethink-room-assignments)), and **28% of Swiss hotels** name employee resistance as a barrier (base: Swiss sub-sample of the HES-SO 2025 survey; team file: ASN-001-25). Competitive pressure should stay unscored (inference): it raises a hotel's wish to adopt, not its ability, so scoring it would push pressured hotels towards "ready" without the foundations. All framework mappings are conceptual; no hotel study has tested this exact question set.

## European hotels stall on knowledge, data and proof of return, not on cost

The best European base is HES-SO's 2025 survey: **about 1,485 hotels in Austria, France, Germany, Greece, Italy and Switzerland, January–April 2025, 82% independent, median 31 rooms** (the number answering each question is not stated). It found **41% use AI**, and the barriers were poor knowledge of the solutions on offer (**39%**; France 58%), high set-up cost (**35%, down from 61% in the 2023 survey**, which covered five countries), technical complexity (**34%**), integration into existing processes (**34%**) and lack of technical skills (**32%**) (team file: ASN-001-15/-16/-17; secondary) ([PhocusWire](https://www.phocuswire.com/european-hotels-show-ai-interest-gaps-remain); [HES-SO news](https://www.hevs.ch/en/news/how-ai-is-and-isn-t-transforming-european-hospitality-212299)). In the follow-up readiness survey (base above: about 80 responses, mostly Swiss), only **9%** had a formal strategy that includes AI and **35%** tested tools case by case; respondents felt confident on integration and staff acceptance but not on data quality or judging return (team file: ASN-002-01/-02/-06).

Chain data is global and vendor-linked, so it should not be compared directly with HES-SO. The h2c AI Opportunity Study 2026 (**122 responses from 113 hotel chains, global, vendor-sponsored, unweighted, Oct 2026**) reports **91%** using AI, **28%** with a senior-led company-wide strategy, **13%** with a measurable return, and self-rated AI know-how of **3.4 out of 10** (team file: FAC-302-03/-09/-11; secondary) ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4134666/new-h2c-study-ai-adoption-is-widespread-among-hotel-chains-but-enterprise-readiness-remains-limited)). Its barrier ranking is reported differently by two sources (integration 38% first in one summary, lack of expertise 56% first in the team's own D7 report), so it should not be cited until checked against the original. The 2025 h2c study (**189 responses from 171 chains, global**) found only **11%** with a dedicated AI budget and integration the top purchase criterion for **70%** (team file: NEX-188-10/-11; secondary) ([PhocusWire](https://www.phocuswire.com/ai-adoption-hotels-independents-large-groups)). State of Hotel AI, fall 2026 (**107 hotel company leaders, global, Aug–Sep 2026, led by management companies 33% and owners 28%**) found only **42%** confident their data is AI-ready, and ranked workflow redesign (**32%**) and experimentation (**34%**) far above executive mandate (**14%**) as success drivers (team file: NEX-081; secondary) ([HOTELS magazine](https://hotelsmag.com/news/hotels-are-using-more-ai-every-day-now-they-need-to-prove-it-pays-off/)). Other bases point the same way: only **24%** of hotels have fully integrated core systems (base: 250+ hotel IT decision makers, global) (secondary) ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/report/4128547/research-fewer-than-one-in-four-hotels-have-fully-integrated-core-systems)), and among EU enterprises with 10+ staff that considered AI but did not adopt it, **48.83%** cited data-protection concerns (base: Eurostat 2025, all sectors; team file: EUR-001-20).

Named cases support the same reading. Accor's SVP Data Science & AI, Nicolas Maynard, is quoted saying more than 90% of hospitality companies pilot AI but only about 30% have embedded it, and that scaling is "a cultural transformation" (expert estimate, base not given; secondary) ([Skift](https://skift.com/2026/09/28/skift-data-ai-summit-europe-preview-accor-nicolas-maynard/)). The team interviews him on 19 November and can ask about the base. Meliá scales nothing without a quantified, validated return and reviews each use case from business, technology, risk, cybersecurity and compliance angles (team file: FAC-199-07/-08); Club Med began by rebuilding its data infrastructure (team file: LNK-002-01).

### What this means for each of the six gates

The evidence supports all six gates in substance, but two are too rigid for the 82%-independent European base (inference from the sources above):

| Gate | Evidence it matters | Research judgement |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** Named owner with time | Central teams plus local champions at Accor and IHG (team file: HYB-070-12, SKF-033-06); 70% of firms with a strategy still call execution fragmented (State of Hotel AI, base above) | **Keep.** Accept "GM or one manager, named, with vendor support" as Yes for small hotels; drop "hours per week in writing" from the proof |
| **F1** 12-month budget, four cost types | 11% of chains have a dedicated AI budget; cost fell from 61% to 35% as a barrier; 58.6% of French hoteliers spend at least EUR 20 a month on AI tools (base: 444 self-selected visitors of Food Hotel Tech 2026; team file: ASN-005-05) | **Keep, make proportional.** Light tools: "known monthly cost and who pays" (merge F4). Moderate/Heavy: full four-part budget. Managed/franchised: accept "funded via system fund or brand programme" |
| **F2** Measured KPI baseline | 13% of chains report a measurable return (h2c 2026); Meliá's rule depends on it | **Keep: strongest gate.** Fold F3 into its proof: "baseline + target + stop date". Give small-hotel examples |
| **T1** Two-way data exchange | Integration top purchase criterion for 70% of chains; 24% fully integrated | **Keep for Moderate/Heavy; not applicable for Light standalone tools** (review replies, content writing). Change proof to "vendor-certified integration with your PMS/POS version plus a sandbox or reference-site test": a full live test is what the pilot is for |
| **D5** Lawful basis + data agreement | GDPR duty whenever a vendor processes personal data; 48.83% Eurostat figure above | **Keep, widen the proof:** signed data-processing agreement, lawful basis recorded, plus clauses on data ownership, export at exit and whether the vendor may train on the data. Say who signs in managed/franchised hotels |
| **O1** Approved one-page plan | Executive mandate 14% vs workflow redesign 32% as success driver | **Keep, light.** Make the one page the same record that holds owner, KPI baseline and stop rule; require the owner's sign-off in managed/franchised hotels (fold in O3) |

Three real blockers are missing and should become **conditional gates**, switched on by the tool's existing risk add-ons rather than asked of every hotel (inference). First, **staff representatives consulted** where the tool touches staff (scheduling, performance, monitoring, kitchen cameras): in Germany the works council co-determines the introduction of any technical device able to monitor staff, under § 87(1) No. 6 BetrVG (secondary) ([CMS](https://cms.law/de/deu/legal-updates/einfuehrung-von-ki-im-unternehmen-einbindung-des-betriebsrats); [Taylor Wessing, Jun 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/06/ki-als-arbeitsmittel-warum-die-mitbestimmung-frueh-mitdenken)), and the AI Act adds a duty to inform workers' representatives before using a high-risk system at work from 2 December 2027 (team file: [EU AI Act hospitality deep dive](EU%20AI%20Act%20hospitality%20deep%20dive.md)). This answers plan v1's open question directly: yes, add it, but only when the staff-facing or sensitive-data add-on applies, because many small hotels have no works council. Other countries' rules (French CSE, Austria, Netherlands) were not checked. Second, a **DPIA** when processing is high-risk (biometrics, profiling, staff monitoring). Third, **human override** (P5, G3) for tools that act on their own, supported by Marriott's failure. AI literacy (AI Act Art. 4) should stay a core question, not a gate, with a result note that doing nothing is non-compliant (team file: deep dive). No published hotel framework with validated gates exists to compare against, so the gate list remains team judgement.

### Chains, independents and managed hotels need different proof, not different gates

Independents lack knowledge and time, pay monthly subscriptions and depend on their platform vendor; HES-SO's advice is plug-and-play tools plus training for small hotels and data governance plus change management for large ones (secondary) ([HES-SO news](https://www.hevs.ch/en/news/how-ai-is-and-isn-t-transforming-european-hospitality-212299)). Chains lack unified data, integration and measured return: only **18% of European chains** have a centralised data structure able to feed AI tools (base: h2c 2025 European sub-sample, via an op-ed) (secondary) ([Hospitality Net, "The Confidence Gap"](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4132652/ai-in-hospitality-the-confidence-gap)). Managed and franchised hotels add a three-way decision between owner, brand and operator; IHG funds major technology through the owner-financed system fund (team file: FAC-272-09), and under the AI Act the deployer stays answerable even when a management company runs oversight (team file: [Hotel AI register design](Hotel%20AI%20register%20design.md)). Evidence on franchised and managed hotels is thin and mostly North American. The practical answer (inference) is to keep one gate list and ask the hotel type once at the start: independents get the subscription and GM-as-owner routes, managed and franchised hotels get owner sign-off and "who is the data controller" in the proof, and chains are reminded that a group-level Yes does not prove readiness at each property.

## Five loopholes let an unready hotel reach "Ready to scale"

The researcher re-ran the tool's own scoring code (copied verbatim from `tools/readiness/index.html` into Node scripts) on **18 constructed profiles across five use cases** and on **20,000 randomly generated hotels**. These are not real hotel answers; they test how the rules behave, not how hotels score.

The current logic is a weakest-link rule (team file). Answers score Yes 2, Partly 1, No 0; N/A and blank answers drop out of both numerator and denominator. Any gate at No, or any area below 40%, gives "Build foundations first"; otherwise the lowest area decides between "Ready to pilot" (40–74%) and "Ready to scale" (75%+). There is no "Not sure" option; the use case's intensity (Light, Moderate, Heavy) never enters the score; every use case shows **36–37 items** (31 checklist questions plus one resource check per area), of which **17–31 are core (mean 21.7)** for the 91 use cases. On clear cases the verdicts are sensible: a chain with strong IT gets "Ready to scale" for dynamic pricing, a hotel answering Partly everywhere gets "Ready to pilot", a strong hotel with no data-processing agreement gets "Build foundations first". Gates drive most outcomes: in the 20,000-hotel simulation, **90% of "Build foundations first" verdicts (13,049 of 14,455) came from a gate at No** (source: tool code, simulation seed 42).

| Loophole | Profile result (constructed) | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A gate at N/A counts as met | Biometric check-in (Heavy, sensitive data) with all six gates N/A and everything else Yes: **"Ready to scale", not even provisional** | No N/A on gates; a gate at N/A or Not sure counts as No |
| A blank gate only makes the result "provisional" | Answering only the six gates, all Yes, on the same Heavy tool: **"Ready to scale (provisional)"** with 19 core items unanswered | Show no verdict until all gates are answered; require at least 80% of core items answered before "Ready to scale" |
| Doubt raises the score | Same hotel leaves 14 uncertain items blank: **Scale (provisional)**; answers them No: **Pilot** | Add "Not sure", score it 0 everywhere, list it as a "find out" action |
| Out-of-scope questions weigh like core ones | Strong hotel, Light review-reply tool, honest No to five non-core questions (group-only and small-hotel-only items): drops from Scale to **Pilot** | Score core items only (or weight core 2, others 1); hide questions whose scope ("Groups", "Managed or franchised") does not match the hotel |
| Gates at Partly reach Scale | All six gates Partly, everything else Yes: **"Ready to scale"** | Require every gate at Yes, with a proof note, for "Ready to scale" |

Two structural issues sit alongside these. The Skills area has only four items, so one No costs **25 points**, against 11 in Organisation; two No answers move a hotel from Scale to Pilot (source: tool code). Scoring core items only would shrink small areas further, so each area should keep at least three scored items (inference). And the verdict hides distance: a hotel averaging **74%** with Organisation at 33% gets "Build foundations first", while one averaging **50%** evenly gets "Ready to pilot". The weakest-link rule is right, since Organisation holds the plan, process redesign and risk review, but the reader needs a number to see how far apart those two hotels are.

### The banded mean is the only formula that never contradicts the verdict

Six candidate formulas were tested for agreement between the score's band (<40 / 40–74 / ≥75) and the verdict (source: tool code, constructed profiles and simulation):

| Formula | Agreement, 18 profiles | Agreement, 20,000 simulated hotels |
|---|---|---|
| Mean of the six areas | 11/18 | 45.2% |
| Lowest area | 17/18 | 87.5% |
| Mean, capped at 39 if a gate is No | 13/18 | 80.2% |
| Mean weighted by core items per area, gate cap | 13/18 | 80.4% |
| Half mean + half lowest, gate cap | 15/18 | 90.5% |
| **Banded mean** | **18/18** | **100%** |

The **banded mean** is the plain average of the six area percentages, rounded, then capped at **39** if any gate is No, N/A, Not sure or unanswered, or any area is below 40%; otherwise capped at **74** if any area is below 75%. It agrees with the verdict by construction and still separates hotels within a band (a strong pricing chain scores 88, an even "Partly" hotel 50). The plain mean fails exactly where it matters: the strong hotel with no data agreement would show **97** next to "Build foundations first". Weighting by intensity added nothing (80.4% vs 80.2%), so intensity should instead raise the bar, for example by requiring all gates at Yes before a Heavy tool can show "Ready to scale" (inference). The screen should show why a score is capped: "39, capped: the data agreement must-have is not met (average of your areas: 97)". The 40% and 75% bands are team judgement and should be checked against the interview hotels, as the tool itself says.

## The changes, in priority order, and what they mean for plan v1

The order below puts first what would otherwise produce a wrong verdict, then what makes the check fair to different hotels, then what makes it more useful. Items 1–6 should be done before the 0–100 score is shown.

| # | Change | Type | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No N/A on gates; a gate at N/A, Not sure or blank counts as No | Scoring | Stress test (Heavy tool reaches Scale with gates N/A) |
| 2 | Add "Not sure", scored 0 in every area, and listed in the result as "Find out: …" | Answer options | Stress test (blanks lift Pilot to Scale); overestimation studies |
| 3 | No verdict until all gates are answered; "Ready to scale" needs ≥80% of core items answered | Scoring | Stress test (gates-only answers reach Scale) |
| 4 | "Ready to scale" needs every gate at Yes with a short proof note; without a note, Yes scores as Partly | Gates, scoring | Stress test; self-assessment bias; Meliá's validated-return rule |

## Conclusion

The research shifts the team's question from "what should the score be?" to "what must a hotel prove before any score is believable?". The number is the easy part, and the banded mean settles it. The harder part is that every established tool, and every self-assessment study found, points to the same risk: hotels, like other organisations, rate themselves too kindly, and a clean 0–100 figure makes that kindness look precise. Treating doubt as a gap, asking for a one-line proof under each must-have, and reserving "Ready to scale" for hotels that can show their evidence turns the check from a quiz into a short audit, which is also what makes it credible to an owner or an HFTP reader.

The second shift is that the check currently measures whether a hotel *can* run an AI tool, not whether its staff and guests *will* accept it, and the team's own Marriott example shows that is where pilots fail. Two short questions repair that. What remains unknown is whether any of this holds on real hotels: the thresholds, the gate list and the five-minute promise are all team judgement. The interview hotels, starting with Accor on 19 November, are the first chance to compare the check's verdict with what actually happened, and that comparison, not more desk research, is what would let the team call the tool validated.
