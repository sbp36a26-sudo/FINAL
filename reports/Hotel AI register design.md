# Build the register hotels cannot buy

EHL SBP Group 36 should build the Hotel AI Register as a free, vendor-neutral list of every AI tool a hotel uses, pre-filled from the team's 91-use catalogue, that gives each tool an AI Act label, the duties and dates that follow, a named responsible person and the evidence to keep, and that prints a one-page summary for owners and auditors. Nothing like it exists for hotels: the market offers enterprise governance platforms priced far above what an independent hotel will pay and free checkers that classify one system at a time and often show out-of-date deadlines, while hospitality bodies have so far published principles, not a record-keeping tool. The reference content is already in the team's files as data, with one large exception: the AI Act label for each of the 91 uses exists only as prose in the governance booklet and must be written out as a 91-row data file and signed off by the D7 owner before anything else is built. The team should build the logic once as plain modules and ship it twice: as a claude.ai artifact with shared storage for the team demo and a small invited pilot, and as a static page that keeps data in the browser and exports files for HFTP members at large, because the artifact platform needs a claude.ai login for every user and stores hotel data in a student's account. The two proposed add-ons are worth building in reduced form. The assistant should be called an "AI Act guide for hotels", never a lawyer, answer only from the team's verified data with the source shown, and exist only in the artifact edition because each question runs on the viewer's own Claude account. The "watch-out radar" should be a dated, sourced change log inside the data file that names which register entries each change affects, not a live news feed, and it is only trustworthy if HFTP names a person who maintains it after hand-over. This report describes the law as the team's files and the research notes recorded it on 17 Nov 2026; it is not legal advice.

*How to read the evidence in this report.* Three marks separate what is known from what is not. **[V]** means verified: read in the primary text, or in the team's own files on 17 Nov 2026. **[S]** means seen only as a search-engine summary of a press release, trade article or blog, because the research sessions could not open most web pages; these must be opened and checked before they are quoted anywhere. **[I]** means the researchers' or this report's own inference or proposal. Statistics carry their source and base (who was surveyed, how many, when) in the same sentence.

## Hotels can choose between a one-off checker and an enterprise platform, with nothing in between

The AI Act does not tell a hotel to keep an AI inventory. A hotel that uses a bought tool is a "deployer" (the booklet's word is "user"): whoever uses an AI system under its own authority ([AI Act Art. 3(4), AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-3)) **[V]**. The only formal registration duty sits with the makers of high-risk systems and with public authorities that use them, not with private businesses such as hotels ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); cited by the researchers from their knowledge of the text, not re-fetched) **[I on the reading; check on EUR-Lex]**. A hotel still cannot meet its duties without a list. It has to know which tools it runs, which label each carries, who uses them, and for any high-risk tool it must follow the maker's instructions, assign human oversight, keep the automatically generated logs for at least six months and inform workers' representatives before use ([AI Act Art. 26](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) **[V]**. Those high-risk duties start on **2 Dec 2027** for the uses listed in the Act's Annex III, which include recruitment and staff management, and on **2 Aug 2028** for AI built into regulated products under Annex I; the duty to tell people they are dealing with AI (Art. 50) has applied since 2 Aug 2026 ([Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj); team data file [ai-act-data.json](../tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json), `timeline`) **[V]**. The team's own booklet already tells every reader to keep such a list: "List every AI tool in use: what it does, who supplies it, who in the hotel is responsible", with head office pooling the lists into a group register ([D7 booklet](../docs/d7-governance/AI_Governance_for_European_Hotels.pdf), "Start here" and B2) **[V]**. The register is the tool that makes the booklet's first instruction doable.

General frameworks agree that an inventory comes first but give no hotel-ready template. The US NIST AI Risk Management Framework asks that "mechanisms are in place to inventory AI systems" (GOVERN 1.6) ([NIST AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/)) **[S; wording to be checked on the NIST page]**. ISO/IEC 42001, the certifiable AI management standard, asks organisations to document the resources, data, impact assessments and lifecycle of each AI system, according to consultancy summaries of the paywalled text ([SureCloud](https://www.surecloud.com/resource-hub/iso-42001-annex-a-controls)) **[S]**. The closest thing hotels already keep is the GDPR record of processing activities: one row per activity with purpose, categories of people and data, recipients, transfers outside the EU, retention and security, shown to the data protection authority on request ([GDPR Art. 30](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)) **[V for the article; field list cited from the researchers' knowledge]**. The register should borrow that row-per-item shape and link each AI tool to its existing GDPR record, so that hotels do not keep two parallel files **[I]**.

Public-sector registers show both a design to copy and a failure to avoid. The UK's Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard has two tiers: a plain-language summary for the public and a detailed tier for experts covering owner, rationale, oversight, data and risks ([GOV.UK ATRS template](https://gov.uk/government/publications/algorithmic-transparency-template)) **[S; an early version of the template was seen]**. The Dutch national algorithm register grew from 123 entries in 2023 to about 1,000, and the government's audit service found that administrative fields are filled in well while substantive fields often fall short of the standard ([Tweede Kamer answer](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/ah-tk-20222023-3133.pdf); [Kamerstuk 26643-1394](https://zoek.officielebekendmakingen.nl/kst-26643-1394.html)) **[S]**. The lesson for a hotel tool is few required fields, drop-downs and pre-filled content instead of free text **[I]**.

The commercial market leaves the middle empty. Enterprise platforms such as Credo AI, Holistic AI and Modulos sell by custom quote; one vendor-comparison blog puts dedicated platforms at about **$50,000 a year** for a focused mid-market deployment (a vendor-attributed claim, [Superblocks](https://www.superblocks.com/blog/ai-governance-platform-pricing)) **[S]**. The Finnish platform Saidot is the only one with quoted prices, and the figures conflict: from **€1,000 a month** (based on a G2 listing, May 2026), a €1,400-a-month plan, or $1,500–3,500 a month, with no prices on its own page in Oct 2026 ([aicompliancevendors.com](https://aicompliancevendors.com/best/eu-ai-act-compliance-tools); [setaicomply.com](https://www.setaicomply.com/resources/best-eu-ai-act-compliance-software-smes)) **[S; all are vendor blogs, directional only]**. At the other end sit free checkers that ask 5 to 13 questions about one system and keep no ongoing register ([euaiact.com](https://www.euaiact.com/eu-ai-act-risk-calculator); [checkcompliance.eu](https://www.checkcompliance.eu/); [eurocomply.app](https://eurocomply.app/eu-ai-act-compliance-checker)) **[S]**. Several of them, and trade articles too, still state that high-risk rules apply from 2 Aug 2026, which the Omnibus changed ([example of the outdated date, Tageskarte](https://www.tageskarte.io/technologie/detail/ai-act-ab-august-2026-was-hotels-und-restaurants-jetzt-kennzeichnen-muessen.html)) **[S]**. None of these tools is specific to hospitality and none ships with a list of hotel uses **[S; the researchers found none, which is not proof that none exists]**.

Hospitality's own bodies have reached principles and coordination, not record-keeping. The AI Hospitality Alliance published a free governance framework in Oct 2026 with principles, a "minimum viable governance" model and a NIST crosswalk ([Lodging Magazine](https://lodgingmagazine.com/ai-hospitality-alliance-publishes-ai-governance-framework/)) **[S; the framework text was not opened, so whether it contains an inventory template is unconfirmed; the team's own AIHA review should settle this]**. HFTP, HSMAI and AHLA/HTNG announced a Global Hospitality AI Coordinating Council on 13 Nov 2026 ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4135455/hftp-hsmai-and-ahlahtng-unite-to-create-global-hospitality-ai-coordinating-council)) **[S]**. No hotel-specific AI register was found, and no survey was found that measures how many European hotels keep an AI inventory or know the AI Act **[S; a gap the team's interviews can fill within their existing guides]**.

The gap the register fills therefore has four parts **[I]**: it recognises the AI hidden inside software a hotel already buys; it translates each tool into an AI Act label and the other laws that bite, in hotel language; it costs nothing; and it separates what the brand, the management company, the owner and the employer are each responsible for. No tool found does any of the four for hotels.

## HFTP's members are controllers and IT directors, and the tool has to reach them without a login

HFTP is a nonprofit founded in 1952 and based in Austin, Texas, serving finance and technology professionals in hotels and private clubs ([Hotel Online, 2026–27 board announcement](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/hftp-announces-2026-2027-global-executive-committee-and-board-of-directors)) **[S]**. Its membership is small and the sources disagree on its size: an undated directory listing says "over 4,600 members", and HFTP's CEO refers to "5,100-plus members" in an undated quote ([Hospitality Net profile](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/association/17004206/hftp); [HFTP membership page](https://www.hftp.org/hospitality_association/interested_in_membership/)) **[S; no 2026 count and no split by country, role or hotel versus club was found]**. Its reach is far wider: HFTP's own figures, quoted in a partnership release around Oct 2026, give **83 chapters in 46 countries and about 100,000 opt-in subscribers** across its publications ([UKTN](https://www.uktech.news/ai/inntelo-ai-signs-deal-to-deploy-ai-agents-across-global-hospitality-association-20261001)) **[S]**. Board lists show controllers, CFOs and IT directors, many from clubs and most from the US **[S]**. Two things follow **[I]**: the tool must speak in terms of cost, control and audit, not legal terms, and it will reach European hotels through HFTP's media and events more than through its member base. HFTP is building its European presence, with an EU Operations Advisor named in Feb 2026 and HITEC Paris announced for 5 Nov 2026 ([Hotel Online](https://www.hotel-online.com/index.php/news/hftp-appoints-lyle-worthington-hftp-global-past-president-as-2026-eu-operations-advisor); [Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4132626/hftp-and-rx-reveals-launch-of-hitec-paris-co-located-with-equiphotel-paris)) **[S; whether HITEC Paris took place and how many attended was not checked]**.

The surveys point to a visibility problem before a compliance problem. In the HES-SO Valais-Wallis survey of European hotels (Schegg, Jan–Apr 2025; about 1,485 hotels in Austria, France, Germany, Greece, Italy and Switzerland, 82% independent, median 31 rooms), 41% used AI, and the top barriers were **poor knowledge of available AI solutions (39%)**, high set-up cost (35%), technical complexity (34%) and lack of technical skills (32%); the number answering the barrier question is not stated ([Schegg 2025](https://hotelinside.ch/wp-content/uploads/PDF-AI-Adoption-in-European-Hotels-2025.pdf); base details from the team's file [readiness/index.html](../tools/readiness/index.html), source S1) **[S for the percentages; the full PDF was not opened]**. A follow-up by Britz and Schegg (Jul–Sep 2025; sent to 408 hoteliers, about 80 responded, 71% Swiss) names lack of time and budget, limited expertise and difficulty evaluating AI providers as the main barriers, and the team's fact base records that 9% have a formal strategy that includes AI (base about 79 hotels) ([Britz & Schegg 2025](https://www.hevs.ch/media/document/22/britz-schegg-2025-ai-readiness-in-hotels.pdf)) **[S]**. Among chains, the vendor-sponsored and unweighted h2c study (Oct 2026; 122 responses from 113 hotel chains, global) reports that 91% use AI, 28% have a senior-led company-wide AI strategy, 13% report a measurable return, and the top barrier is lack of AI expertise, training or skills (56%), ahead of privacy (29%) and data governance (28%) ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4134666/new-h2c-study-ai-adoption-is-widespread-among-hotel-chains-but-enterprise-readiness-remains-limited)) **[S]**. Owners rank differently: in Wyndham's Owner Trends survey (Nov–Dec 2025; 325 hotel owners and developers in the US, Canada and the Caribbean, so not European), data privacy and security came first at 46%, then cost at 42% ([Wyndham release](https://corporate.wyndhamhotels.com/news-releases/hotel-owners-at-an-ai-crossroads-as-confidence-and-growth-plans-hold-firm-wyndham-owner-trends-report-finds/)) **[S]**.

Read together **[I]**, these say that a hotel's first need is to see what AI it already runs, that the person filling the register has little time and no AI training, and that owners want a one-page view of risk and cost per property. The register should therefore be fillable in under an hour by a controller or general manager, start from a pre-filled list by department, record cost and contract end date next to each tool so that finance gets the AI budget line most chains lack, and never demand an account. These are design hypotheses to test with interview hotels, not findings.

HFTP's AI structure gives the tool a natural home. HFTP launched an AI Council in Feb 2026, renamed it the AI Collective at HITEC in June 2026 with five subcommittees including AI Ethics and Governance, announced an industry AI portal "in development", and named a gap analysis as the first step of its governance work ([HITEC.org](https://www.hitec.org/news/hftp-ai-collective-unveils-strategic-roadmap-at-hitec-2026-including-thought-leadership-certificate-and-standards); [Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4133425/hftps-ai-collective-solidifies-roadmap-to-provide-industry-wide-resources)) **[S]**. No published HFTP governance template was found, and the HFTP material seen is US-led and does not cover the AI Act **[S]**. The pitch to the client is that the register is the Europe-specific, practical layer under the Collective's governance work, to be listed in the AI portal and owned by the Ethics and Governance subcommittee **[I]**. Reach then runs through four routes **[I]**: an open page linked from HFTP's publications (Hotel Online, Hospitality Upgrade), which reaches the subscriber base instead of only members; a session at the Hospitality AI Exchange planned for 2–3 Feb 2027 or the next HITEC Paris ([Asian Hospitality](https://www.asianhospitality.com/hftp-hsmai-ahla-global-hospitality-ai-council/)) **[S]**; an HFTP Academy micro-module built around filling in the register; and the D9 and D10 deliverables, which can carry it. HFTP and its partners describe their AI work as "no-sales" guidance for professionals "not sales targets" **[S]**, so the tool must carry no vendor logo, list vendors only as examples, and ship as "prepared for HFTP by EHL SBP Group 36" until HFTP decides on its own branding. Who owns the deliverable is set by the EHL project agreement, which the researchers could not see; the team should ask the coach and record the answer as a written scope item.

## One row per tool, with separate fields for the user, the employer and the party that chose it

The register does eight things, in this order of importance **[I]**. It lists every AI tool by department and outlet. It labels each tool under the AI Act, using the booklet's vocabulary. It generates the duties and the dates that follow from the label. It names one responsible person per tool. It records who is the user, who is the maker, who employs the affected staff and who decided on the tool. It holds the evidence a hotel would be asked for. It flags entries when the law or guidance changes. And it exports a one-page summary per property plus a file backup. It gives "items to check", never a verdict that a hotel is compliant.

The label vocabulary comes from the booklet: Prohibited, High-risk, "High-risk if…", Transparency and Minimal, plus "outside the high-risk list" for tools the Act excludes but other law covers strictly, each with a mark where it rests on draft guidance, the team's own reading, or a contested point ([D7 booklet](../docs/d7-governance/AI_Governance_for_European_Hotels.pdf), A2) **[V]**. The booklet's count gives a sense of proportion: of the 91 uses on the team's list, 3 are high-risk as normally used and 13 more become high-risk depending on set-up, 11 of those 16 concern staff, 5 can become prohibited through a single feature, and 11 carry a duty to tell people they are dealing with AI or to label realistic AI images (the team's own classification of use descriptions, not of named products; D7 booklet, A6) **[V that the booklet says this; the classification itself is the team's reading]**. The practical meaning is that most hotel tools need a notice and a named owner, and the heavy duties cluster around staff.

| Field group | What the hotel records | Where the content comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Tool | Department, outlet, catalogue use or custom description, vendor and product, how it arrived (bought, bundled in a suite, switched on by a vendor update, built in-house, personal tool used for work) | Catalogue: 15 departments, 91 uses, 152 vendors **[V]**; "how it arrived" is new **[I]** |
| Label | AI Act label, mark (draft guidance, our reading, contested), the condition that would change it, date it applies from | New label file from booklet A6 and Part C; dates from `timeline` **[V sources; file to be written]** |
| Roles | User (deployer), maker (provider) and by which route, who decided (property, brand, head office), employer of affected staff, GDPR role | Booklet A3 and A4 **[V]** |
| Duties | Generated list with due date, status and owner | `obligationsByRoleAndTier`, AI literacy, Art. 50, GDPR layer **[V]** |
| Evidence | Impact assessment (DPIA) done, staff representatives consulted, staff briefed, seven vendor answers, AI notice checked, logs kept six months, link to GDPR record | Booklet A3, B1–B3; readiness checklist of 31 questions **[V]** |
| Other law | Tags: Labour, GDPR, Consumer, Competition, Liability, Cyber/PCI, CH, UK | Booklet A8 **[V]** |
| Money and status | Cost, contract end date, status (proposed, pilot, live, paused, retired), review date | New **[I]** |

Operating models are where a generic register fails and a hotel one earns its place. The Commission's Art. 50 guidelines of 5 Nov 2026 say that the deployer is whoever decides to use the system and how, that technical control is not needed, and that this holds inside group structures (C(2026) 7702, as recorded in [ai-act-data.json](../tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json), `transparencyGuidelines` t1) **[V in the team's data; exact wording to be re-checked in the guideline before quoting]**. The booklet turns this into a table, which is the team's reading and not a Commission table:

| Set-up | Usually the user (deployer) | What to watch | What the register adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner-operated | The operating company | Tools chosen by individual departments | One list, one responsible person per tool |
| Managed | The management company | The brand, where its standards impose a tool and how it is used; the owner usually is not the user | Periodic extract to the owner; a copy for the owner at the end of the contract |
| Franchised | The franchisee | The brand, where standards impose the tool and its settings | "Decided by brand" flag; the property's own list of local tools |
| Leased | The tenant that operates the hotel | A tenant-run restaurant inside the hotel is its own user and employer | Recorded as a third-party outlet, not as the hotel's tool |

Source: [D7 booklet](../docs/d7-governance/AI_Governance_for_European_Hotels.pdf), A4 **[V that the booklet says this]**; right-hand column **[I]**.

Three facts make separate fields necessary. Duties towards staff, such as consultation and data protection, fall on the company that employs them, which can differ from the AI Act user (D7 booklet, A4) **[V]**. GDPR roles between owner, manager and brand are disputed in practice: the law firm CMS reports that some hotel operators deny any controller-to-processor or joint-controller relationship while others accept one and amend their contracts ([CMS, "Hotels and GDPR"](https://cms.law/content/download/333455/file/Hotels%20GDPR.pdf)) **[S]**. And no law-firm source was found that writes AI Act roles into management or franchise agreements, so who is the user under a given contract is an open legal question for each hotel **[S; a gap]**. A managed or franchised hotel can therefore have three different companies as AI Act user, GDPR controller and employer, and the register must hold all three per tool instead of one "deployer" field per hotel **[I]**.

For groups the register works in two tiers **[I]**. Brands already control the core systems through approved-vendor lists and waivers: IHG approved Oracle OPERA Cloud as a property management system for its Americas and EMEAA estates in Jan 2026 ([Hospitality Net](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4130667.html)) **[S]**, and US franchise disclosure documents show approved lists with waiver routes ([Crowne Plaza FDD 2025](https://www.fdd.ninja/brands/crowne_plaza/2025/designates_acceptable_thirdparty_vendors_propertylevel_technology)) **[S; US evidence only]**. The central tier is an approved list kept by brand or group IT, with the vendor checks done once. The property tier records which approved tools are switched on, with local settings, local owner and local staff consultation, plus anything chosen locally. The waiver request is the natural moment to create an entry for a non-standard tool. For owners, who are rarely the user but carry the money risk, the register is an asset record: an extract belongs in due diligence when a hotel is sold, re-branded or changes operator, and the booklet already advises that management and franchise agreements state who bears the cost of compliance and of fines (D7 booklet, A4 and B1) **[V]**.

The blind spots are tools nobody bought. Oracle added AI features to OPERA Cloud for existing customers at no extra cost on 16 Jun 2026, including a staff assistant and room-assignment recommendations ([Oracle press release](https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/new-ai-capabilities-in-oracle-opera-cloud-supercharge-hotel-operations-2026-06-16/)) **[S]**, so a hotel can acquire AI without a purchase. Staff also use general chatbots on their own; no hotel-specific survey quantifying this was found, and the team's data file lists it as an open item **[V]**. In France, approving a chatbot that staff already use, or writing a charter for it, counts as introducing it and requires prior consultation of the staff committee, according to a Paris appeal court ruling of 21 May 2026 cited in the booklet **[V that the booklet cites it]**. The "how it arrived" field and a monthly look at vendor release notes turn these into register entries **[I]**.

In-hotel F&B shows why the AI Act label is not the most useful field everywhere. The booklet labels ordering bots and table-booking phone agents as Transparency, talking service robots as Transparency if not obviously a machine, food-waste cameras as Minimal but High-risk if footage or logs are used to rate individual cooks (draft guidance), smart ovens as Minimal for the hotel with a question for the maker from 2 Aug 2028, and menu pricing, forecasting and recipe tools as Minimal (D7 booklet, C4) **[V]**. The weight comes from other laws. In Germany the works council co-decides on any technical device suitable for monitoring staff, whether or not monitoring is intended ([Taylor Wessing, 24 Jun 2026](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/06/ki-als-arbeitsmittel-warum-die-mitbestimmung-frueh-mitdenken)) **[S; its application to kitchen cameras is inferred, no ruling was found]**. The food business remains responsible for allergen information whatever tool produced the answer ([Regulation (EU) 1169/2011](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj)) **[V]**. Card numbers given to a phone agent bring it into payment-card security scope (D7 booklet, citing PCI DSS v4.0.1) **[V that the booklet says this]**. For F&B outlets the register should therefore ask five plain questions **[I]**: can it see or measure staff, does it touch card data, does it answer allergen or nutrition questions, is it a connected product, and did the hotel build or rebrand it. Kitchen tools are often bought by the chef or arrive inside equipment, which makes them the entries most likely to be missing from a group list; recording the outlet (restaurant, bar, room service, banqueting, kitchen) lets outlet managers see their own.

For multi-country groups each entry stores the regime that applies. Switzerland and the UK have no AI act; the Swiss data protection act and the UK GDPR apply, and the AI Act still reaches a Swiss or UK group whose AI output is used in the EU ([ai-act-data.json](../tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json), `nationalLayer`) **[V in the team's data]**. The register should show "AI Act: does not apply here; Swiss or UK data protection and labour rules do" instead of a blank, and attach the staff-consultation step that fits the country, since the data file's key finding is that labour law, not AI Act enforcement, is the sharper national constraint for hotels **[V in the team's data]**.

## Most of the content exists as data; the 91 labels do not

The team's files were read on 17 Nov 2026 and contain nearly everything a register needs **[V]**. The catalogue ([use-case-catalogue/index.html](../tools/use-case-catalogue/index.html)) holds 15 departments, 91 uses with task, AI function, adoption level and sources, 152 vendors and 41 hotel groups. The readiness tool ([readiness/index.html](../tools/readiness/index.html)) adds, for each of the 91 uses, integration effort, one of nine tool types, flags for sensitive personal data (8 uses), payment data (3), hardware (13) and acting without human sign-off (10), and a checklist of 31 questions of which six are gates. The AI Act data file ([ai-act-data.json](../tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json), version 1.1, facts verified 10 Nov 2026) holds 37 sections including the timeline, duties by role and label, 13 worked scenarios, a GDPR layer, a national layer for six countries, open items, and an eight-question classification wizard that is pure data. The booklet supplies the seven vendor questions, the operating-model table and the eight other-law tags, which need transcribing.

Three defects must be fixed before building **[V for the facts, I for the fixes]**. First, the label of each use is not stored as data anywhere: the booklet publishes department-level groupings and the totals (3, 13, 5, 11), and the original 91-row classification worksheet is not in the repository. The D7 owner must write a label file with one row per use (label, mark, the conditions that change it, Art. 50 duties, other-law tags, booklet reference), ideally from the original worksheet instead of reverse-engineering the PDF, and a small script must check that the file reproduces the booklet's four totals. A researcher's draft mapping reached the 3 high-risk uses but could not confirm the other totals row by row. Second, uses have no stable identifier: codes such as `hr-2` exist only in the readiness data and are positional, so inserting one use in the catalogue would silently shift every code, every label and every saved register entry. The team should freeze an identifier table and validate it at build time. Third, the vocabularies differ: the booklet has five labels plus "outside the high-risk list", while the data file and wizard have four tiers. "High-risk if…" should be stored as a base label plus conditions expressed as wizard answers, so that ticking "ranks staff on ratings" re-runs the same logic the explorer uses.

The wizard can classify a custom tool that is not in the catalogue. Its `walk`, `collect` and `resolveTier` functions in the explorer are small and generic, and the researchers recommend lifting them into a shared module that can be tested outside the browser (described from reading the code; the engine was not run against test inputs) **[V for the code reading]**. It has limits a register must cover with extra data **[V]**: it knows only "bought from a vendor", "built" and "pilot", with no route for a tool imposed by a brand or supplied by head office; it has no Swiss or UK branch; and it outputs no other-law tags beyond GDPR. The 13 scenarios in the data file make ready test cases: the module must reproduce each scenario's tier from some set of answers.

Hotel data and reference data should be kept apart **[I]**. Reference data (catalogue, readiness, AI Act, labels, booklet tables, watch log) is read-only, versioned and bundled into the page by a build script, as the team's hub already does, which also ends today's drift risk from three copies of the catalogue and two of the AI Act data. Hotel data is small: one organisation record, one record per property (country, operating model, operator, brand, employer, staff representation, outlets), one record per tool with the fields in the table above, and a change history. Each tool entry stores the answers that produced its label and the version of the reference data it was classified with, mirroring the team's Assessment tool, which pins a framework version on every record and offers to re-score **[V for the precedent]**. Duties are generated from the data, never typed.

The platform question has a clear answer once the constraints are set side by side. They come from the claude.ai artifact capability definitions (`db.d.ts`, `user.d.ts`, `downloads.d.ts`, read on 17 Nov 2026 in the session's bundled skill files) and from the team's Assessment tool documentation ([assessment/README.md](../tools/assessment/README.md)) **[V]**: every visitor needs a claude.ai sign-in to see any data; people invited from outside the owner's organisation are either read-only or full Editors who can also publish new versions of the page; sharing by public link stops invited Editors from saving; data lives in one owner's artifact and is erased with it; the last save wins on concurrent edits and no history is kept; a database holds at most 25,000 records of 256 KiB each; per-user privacy is a rule, not encryption, and the owner can loosen it without anyone being told; and, by the team's own finding, saving only works on an artifact's main page, so the register cannot sit as a tab inside the current hub.

| Option | Use it for | Why not for everything |

Whether real hotel records may sit in a student-owned artifact, even for a pilot, is a question for the client and coach, and the project plan requires scope changes to be validated in writing.

## The guide may explain the team's data; it must not rule on a hotel's case

The user's idea of a "personal lawyer chatbot" answers a real need: the surveys above put skills and knowledge at the top of the barrier list, and a controller facing "Transparency, draft guidance" will want to ask what that means for the restaurant's booking line. The name is the first thing to change **[I]**. "Lawyer" and "personal" promise advice on an individual case, which the team cannot give, which the booklet and data file expressly disclaim **[V]**, and which would sit badly with HFTP's stated neutrality and with any association's caution about what it publishes. "AI Act guide for hotels" describes what the feature is: a way to ask questions of the booklet and the data in plain words. Its button should say "Ask the guide", and its first line on screen should say that it explains the team's research, is not legal advice, and can be wrong.

The platform sets hard limits, read from the capability definition (`sample.d.ts`, 17 Nov 2026) **[V]**. A published page can ask Claude a question, but each call spends the viewer's own Claude usage, the viewer is asked to allow it at the first call in a view, and a decline switches the feature off for that view. Each call is memory-less: Claude sees only the text the page sends, cannot browse the web, and the page controls no system prompt, so the instruction, the data and the output format must all travel in the request. A request can carry at most 256 KiB of text; the AI Act data file alone is 190,191 bytes (measured), so the page cannot simply attach every data file and must select the relevant sections. Where the page runs outside a Claude viewer, including any saved or static copy, the feature does not exist. The consequences are direct **[I]**: the guide lives only in the artifact edition; the static member edition replaces it with a searchable list of prepared questions and answers; and the design must work with the guide absent.

Grounding strictly in verified data is achievable in this design, with one caveat **[I]**. The page picks the sections that match the question and the open register entry (the tool's label row, the matching duties, the relevant booklet section, the country entry, any watch-log items that touch it), sends them with a fixed instruction to answer only from that material, to cite the section or article for every statement, to repeat the confidence mark (settled, scheduled, judgement, draft guidance, contested, which the data file already carries **[V]**), and to say "the team's data does not cover this; ask counsel" when the material is silent. The page then shows the source passages next to the answer so the reader can check one against the other. The caveat is that a language model can still misstate a source it was given; showing the passages and testing a fixed set of questions before each release (the 13 scenarios supply them) reduces this and does not remove it. The guide should never output a verdict such as "you are compliant" or "this tool is legal"; its closing line is always a short list of things to check or people to ask.

Placement follows from that **[I]**. The main entry point is on each tool's result card, as "Explain this label", "What would change it?" and "What do I ask the vendor?", where the page already knows which data to send. A general "Ask the guide" box sits on the booklet and timeline views. It does not sit on the home screen and is not the product's headline; the register is.

Three limits must be stated on screen and in the hand-over note **[I]**. Staleness: the guide knows nothing newer than the data file, so every answer carries the "facts verified on" date and any watch-log item that touches the question, and it must say so when asked about something that changed after that date. No personal data: the page sends the tool, label and country, never the responsible person's name, staff or guest details, and the input box tells users not to type names or case details; the platform's own rule for stored user data is to keep identifiers only **[V]**. Cost and access: only viewers with a Claude account can use it and it draws on their own usage, which is acceptable for a team demo and an invited pilot and is a reason not to promise it to HFTP's wider audience. HFTP already runs AI agents from its partner Inntelo AI across its websites and events ([Hotel Online](https://www.hotel-online.com/news/hftp-names-inntelo-ai-as-ai-partner-deploying-ai-agents-for-members-media-and-events)) **[S]**; whether those agents can take in the team's question-and-answer content is unknown and worth asking the client, because that would be the route to members who have no Claude account.

## The radar is a dated change log in the data file, not a news feed

The second idea, a radar that tracks changes to the AI Act and related guidance, is the stronger of the two add-ons because the register's labels rest on moving ground and the team has already done most of the work. The booklet ends with a "What is still moving" table of eight items with a "who should watch" column, and the data file holds open items that each list what they affect, interim positions with a stated trigger and an "if it changes" instruction, and a nine-date timeline **[V]**. What is missing is the link from each change to the register entries it touches, and a routine for keeping the list current.

A live feed is not possible on the artifact platform and would not be trustworthy if it were **[V for the first half, I for the second]**. The page's call to Claude cannot browse, so a published page cannot fetch news by itself; and unreviewed headlines are exactly the kind of content that put wrong dates into the free checkers. The radar should therefore be a `watch` section in the data file with two lists. The first holds the standing items to watch; the second is an append-only change log. Each change-log entry carries the date of the event, what changed in one plain sentence, the status (proposal, draft, final, in force, court ruling), a link to the primary document, the confidence mark, the sections of the data file and the scenario and use identifiers it affects, the action for hotels, who should watch, and the date and name of the person who verified it **[I]**. Because every register entry stores the data version it was classified with, the page can then show "2 of your tools are affected by a change since you last reviewed" and take the user to them, which is the feature no checker offers. The radar works identically in the artifact and the static edition, since it is only data.

The starting contents come straight from the team's files:

Feeding the radar is a two-step routine **[I]**. Detection can be cheap: a fixed list of official pages to check (the Commission's AI Act Service Desk and its list of market surveillance authorities, EUR-Lex, the Court of Justice case page, the Swiss Federal Council and the UK regulator), on a monthly cycle and around the known dates. The team already runs scheduled review agents on weekday mornings, and a similar scheduled check can draft proposed entries. Publication is the human step that makes it trustworthy: nothing enters the log until a named person has opened the primary document, written the entry, set the affected identifiers and dated it. Entries are never edited after publication, only superseded, and trade-press items never enter without the primary source. The data file's own asymmetric rule is the right editorial policy: apply draft readings that widen a hotel's exposure now, and do not rely on readings that narrow it until they are final **[V in the team's data]**.

Who maintains it after hand-over decides whether the radar is an asset or a liability. The honest design assumes maintenance can stop **[I]**. The page shows "watch list last checked on" prominently; once that date is older than a set period, say 90 days, the banner changes to "not checked since…" and labels that rest on draft guidance display a warning. The hand-over package names the maintainer, most plausibly the AI Collective's Ethics and Governance subcommittee or a person it designates, the checking routine, the source list and the time it takes. If HFTP names nobody, the team should ship the radar as a frozen "what was still moving at hand-over" list with its date and say so plainly, instead of a feature that looks live and is not.

## The main risks are a wrong label read as advice and a tool nobody owns

The risks sort by how much damage they do **[I]**. The most serious is liability perception: a screen that says "Minimal" next to a member's tool reads as legal advice. The defences are the booklet's own wording that labels cover uses as described and not named products, visible marks for draft and contested points, "items to check" instead of verdicts, the date on every screen and export, and a link to the official text. The second is legal drift, which the radar addresses and which is certain, not possible: the final high-risk guidelines are expected to move some "High-risk if…" rows. The third is orphaning after the project ends, which only a named HFTP owner solves. The fourth is completeness: the Dutch experience suggests registers fill up with names and stay empty on substance, so the tool should keep required fields few and count substantive ones. The fifth is data: the positional identifiers, the copies of the catalogue, last-save-wins storage and data that disappears with an artifact are all known and all fixable in the build. The sixth is trust in the opposite direction: a written record of AI use could feel like evidence against the hotel, a fear the researchers flag without survey support; the static edition, where the file never leaves the hotel, is the answer. Finally, the evidence base of this report is itself a risk: most market, membership and survey findings are search summaries, and the pages must be opened before any figure reaches a client-facing page.

Success should be measured at three levels, and since no benchmark for hotel AI registers was found, every target below is a proposal to agree with the coach and client **[I]**. Build quality has objective tests: the label file reproduces the booklet's four totals, the classification module reproduces the 13 scenarios, and the static edition runs offline. Usability is tested with interview and pilot hotels: a general manager or controller completes a first register for one property in under an hour without help, finds at least one AI tool the hotel did not know it had, and can say what one label means and what to do next; the share of substantive fields filled (label conditions, responsible person, evidence) is the number to watch. Adoption and durability are HFTP's measures: the tool is listed in the AI portal or linked from an HFTP publication, HFTP has named a maintainer and the watch list has been checked at least once after hand-over, and downloads or opens are counted from whichever channel HFTP uses. The team's interviews, starting with Accor on 19 Nov 2026, can test the demand side within the existing guides: whether a group keeps any AI list today and who would fill one.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the project in two ways. The register is less a legal tool than a visibility tool with a legal layer: for most hotels its first result will be the discovery that AI is already running inside the property management system, the booking line and the kitchen, and that the duties attached are mostly a notice, a named person and a conversation with staff representatives. That is a message HFTP's finance and technology audience can act on, and it is why the pre-filled catalogue, not the classifier, is the feature competitors lack. And the hard part is not software. The team can build the MVP from existing data in weeks, but its value over time depends on three things outside the code: a signed-off label for each of the 91 uses, a named owner at HFTP, and a watch list that is honest about the day it was last checked.

The two add-ons test the same principle from different sides. The guide is useful exactly as far as it stays inside the team's verified data and shows its sources, and the radar is trustworthy exactly as far as a person stands behind each dated entry. A register that says what it does not know, when it was last checked and who to ask next is a stronger deliverable for a neutral association than one that appears to know everything, and it is the version the team can defend in front of both AI experts and hoteliers who are new to the subject.
