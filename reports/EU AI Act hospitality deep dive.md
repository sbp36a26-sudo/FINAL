# AI Act exits narrow under hotel scrutiny

For a hotel that buys its AI tools from vendors, the EU AI Act today imposes four live duties and one deferred regime: do not use a prohibited practice (Art. 5, since 2 Feb 2025), take measures to support staff AI literacy (Art. 4, since 2 Feb 2025), tell people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation (Art. 50(3), since 2 Aug 2026), and label deep fakes and unreviewed public-interest text (Art. 50(4), since 2 Aug 2026); the high-risk regime for staff and biometric tools starts only on **2 December 2027 (Annex III)** and **2 August 2028 (Annex I products)** ([Omnibus Art. 1(40), p.35](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)). Exposure concentrates in three places: tools that select, rank, score or watch staff and candidates (Annex III point 4), biometrics and emotion reading (Annex III point 1 and the workplace ban in Art. 5(1)(f)), and AI that talks to guests or produces realistic content (Art. 50). Of the 68 exits and grey areas consolidated here from five researchers and three reviewers, the ones that hold, outright or on conditions the hotel can evidence, are written into an article and rest on a provable fact: rules-only software is not AI, one-to-one face verification is outside Annex III, pricing is not on the high-risk list, machinery-safety AI is regulated through machinery law, and private hotels owe no fundamental-rights impact assessment. The ones that fail depend on a label, a contract clause, a corporate structure, a location or company size: "a manager signs off", "it is only a pilot", "the vendor says it is not for HR", "we are too small", "head office is abroad". The largest real exit, the legacy rule in Art. 111(2), relieves the hotel as well as the vendor for a tool genuinely in service before 2 Dec 2027 and unchanged since, but its extension to later purchases rests on a recital only and it never covers the bans, the notices, data protection or labour law. In almost every case where an AI Act exit holds, the GDPR, national labour law or consumer law already catches the same conduct, and those are the channels with a fining record in hospitality. The team's booklet, data file, revision list and catalogue are accurate on the main lines; this report lists 38 corrections, led by two "new in the Omnibus" claims that are in fact 2024 text, a present-tense warning about becoming a tool's "maker" that cannot yet apply, and a headline count of 13 "high-risk if" uses that is 11 on a strict match with the catalogue. This is research for compliance planning by a student team; it is not legal advice.

## How to read this report: five evidence marks, kept apart

Every finding carries one of five marks, because the layers differ sharply in reliability. **[T]** means the wording was read in one of the two regulation PDFs held by the team: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 ([the Act](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj), 144 pages, OJ 12.7.2024) and Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 ([the Omnibus](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj), 41 pages, OJ 24.7.2026, in force 27 July 2026). Page numbers are PDF pages of those files. **[G]** means a final Commission guideline that no researcher could open: its content is known only through the team's data file `ai-act-data.json` or through web-search summaries of law-firm articles. This covers the guidelines on prohibited practices, on the AI-system definition, on general-purpose AI models and on Art. 50 transparency. **[D]** means the Commission's draft high-risk classification guidelines of 19 May 2026, also unopened, known only through the team's data file; they are a draft, not adopted, and not binding. **[S]** means secondary commentary or trade press seen as a search summary. **[I]** means a researcher's or reviewer's own inference. Commission guidelines of any kind do not bind courts; only the Court of Justice gives an authoritative reading.

The research was produced in two rounds. Five researchers wrote notes on scope, prohibited practices, high-risk classification and duties, transparency and enforcement, and use-case coverage. Three reviewers then attacked them. A fact-checker re-read both regulation texts on 21 disputed points and confirmed 17 outright, found four partly right or not checkable from the local texts, and found no misquotation of the regulation in any note ([fact-check note, summary table](../research_notes/EU%20AI%20Act%20hospitality%20deep%20dive/challenge_fact_check.md)); its verdicts are treated here as the most reliable layer on what the text says. An "enforcer" reviewer argued every exit from the side of a market surveillance authority, a data protection authority, a works council and a consumer plaintiff, and downgraded seven "safe" ratings ([enforcer note](../research_notes/EU%20AI%20Act%20hospitality%20deep%20dive/challenge_enforcer_view.md)). A "hotel counsel" reviewer looked for lawful room the researchers missed and for over-caution, and found four unused provisions and three habits of over-statement ([hotel-counsel note](../research_notes/EU%20AI%20Act%20hospitality%20deep%20dive/challenge_hotel_counsel_view.md)). Where they disagree, both views are shown. Statements by the reviewers about the GDPR, consumer directives, competition-law case law and national labour law were made from memory and are marked for verification at the end.

Three verdict words are used throughout. **HOLDS** means the exit rests on operative text and on a fact the hotel can prove. **HOLDS ONLY IF** means it works under stated conditions, or rests on a recital, a guideline or a draft. **FAILS** means a regulator or court would reject it. **OPEN** marks points the text does not decide and no authority has yet addressed. "Holds" always means "under the AI Act"; it never means lawful overall.

## Every EU hotel is in scope, but only four duties bite before December 2027

### No size threshold, no sector exclusion, and roles follow facts

The Act applies to every "deployer" established in the Union, meaning anyone "using an AI system under its authority" in a professional activity, and to every "provider" that places a system on the EU market or puts it into service, wherever that provider sits ([Act Art. 2(1), p.45; Art. 3(3)–(4), p.46](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. There is **no employee, revenue or room-count threshold anywhere in Art. 2**: a 12-room family hotel using a chatbot is a deployer [T, scope note]. Company size changes only fine ceilings and some paperwork for makers of high-risk systems. Non-EU operators are reached "where the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union" (Art. 2(1)(c)); recital 22 says "intended to be used", and the fact-checker confirmed that this mismatch between article and recital is real and unresolved ([Act p.45, p.6](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T].

Roles are assigned by function, not by contract label or by the hotel industry's owner, operator, brand and franchisee split. The provider is whoever develops a system, or has one developed, and puts it into service "under its own name or trademark", including "for own use" ([Act Art. 3(3), 3(11), pp.46–47](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. A group that builds a guest assistant on a language-model API is therefore a provider of that assistant, not a "mere user" [T + I]. Neither "under its authority" nor "undertaking" is defined, and no researcher found any guidance on who is the deployer in a managed or franchised hotel, where an owner employs the staff, an operator runs the tool and a brand mandates it; the Commission's value-chain guidelines on Art. 25 are announced but unpublished [I; D for the announcement]. This is the largest structural gap for hotels and it is not an exit for anyone: an authority can treat more than one company as deployer.

### What is an "AI system", and what is not

The definition turns on inference: a machine-based system that "infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions" ([Act Art. 3(1), p.46](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The only exclusion written by the legislator sits in recital 12: systems "based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations". The same recital names "logic- and knowledge-based approaches" as AI techniques, and adaptiveness is optional, so a model trained once and frozen is still AI ([Act recital 12, p.4](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. Finer exclusions (basic data processing, simple prediction, mathematical optimisation) come from Commission guidelines seen only in summary [G] ([Covington summary](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/european-commission-guidelines-on-the-definition-of-an-ai-system/)). In hotel terms: PMS automation rules, rate fences typed in by a revenue manager, fixed-formula tip pools and thermostat schedules are outside; a machine-learned demand forecast, a language-model chatbot, face matching and CV ranking are inside; a "scripted" chatbot that uses a learned intent classifier is inside; a constraint-solver rota is genuinely grey [I]. Being outside the definition removes every AI Act duty at once, including literacy and chatbot disclosure, and removes nothing under the GDPR, labour law or consumer law.

### What a typical vendor-tool hotel owes in November 2026

The hotel-counsel reviewer sorted the booklet's advice into legal duty, other law, and prudence, and the sorting stands on the text. Under the AI Act a hotel that only deploys vendor tools owes today: no prohibited practice (Art. 5); measures to support AI literacy (Art. 4); a notice to people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorisation (Art. 50(3)); and a visible label on deep fakes and on unreviewed AI text about matters of public interest (Art. 50(4)) [T]. The chatbot notice in Art. 50(1) and the machine-readable marking in Art. 50(2) are addressed to "Providers", confirmed by the fact-checker ([Act Art. 50, p.82](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. A tool inventory, training records, checking that the vendor's chatbot notice is switched on, and asking for CE-mark confirmation are prudent and cheap, and they build the evidence that Art. 99(7) rewards, but the Act does not prescribe them for a deployer [T + I]. Involving a works council before introducing a staff tool, running a data protection impact assessment and giving candidates information about automated decisions are duties today under other laws, not under the AI Act [I, from memory, to verify].

## Prohibited practices: one live ban for hotels, and a blind spot on guest scoring

### Emotion recognition at work is the ban a hotel can breach by accident

Art. 5(1)(f) bans "the use of AI systems to infer emotions of a natural person in the areas of workplace and education institutions", except systems for "medical or safety reasons" ([Act p.51](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The fine tier is the top one: up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; SMEs get "whichever is lower", small mid-caps do not get that relief for Art. 5 ([Act Art. 99(3), (6), pp.115–116; Omnibus Art. 99(6a), p.35](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)) [T, confirmed by the fact-checker]. Use is banned without the qualifier "for this specific purpose" that protects the vendor's placing on the market, so a hotel that switches on an agent-emotion module in a general analytics suite is exposed even where the vendor is not [T + I]. The most likely real breach is not a "mood camera" but a contact-centre or conversation-intelligence product with an "agent sentiment", "empathy score" or "tone analytics" feature enabled by default [I, prohibited-practices note]. The coverage audit found the same switch unflagged in the booklet for the IT service desk (catalogue use 84, which lists "sentiment, speech analytics" on staff calls) and for meeting tools [I].

Four things the text settles: there is no consent exception; the label a vendor gives the feature is irrelevant; the legacy rule does not protect it (Art. 111(2) is "without prejudice to the application of Article 5"); and the ban applies to "a natural person in the areas of workplace", not only to "employees", so agency banqueting staff and trainees should be treated as covered [T + I]. Hotel schools and academies are "education institutions", so reading students' emotions is banned on the second limb [I].

Three things the text does not settle, all confirmed by the fact-checker. First, **the word "biometric" is not in Art. 5(1)(f)**; it sits in the definition of "emotion recognition system" (Art. 3(39)), which the prohibition does not use, and in recital 44. Second, **job candidates are not named** in Art. 5(1)(f) or its recitals; the word "candidates" appears once in the Act, in Annex III point 4(a). That recruitment is inside "the areas of workplace" comes from the Commission's guidelines on prohibited practices [G] ([Lewis Silkin summary](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/02/17/understanding-the-eu-ai-acts-prohibited-practices-key-workplace-and-advertising-102k011)). Third, whether a guest standing in a hotel (the staff's workplace) is "in the areas of workplace" is not answered by the text; the guidelines, as summarised, treat a call centre tracking its customers' emotions as outside the ban provided employees' emotions are not tracked at the same time [G] ([Covington summary](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/european-commission-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices-under-the-eu-artificial-intelligence-act/)). Guest emotion reading is therefore regulated by Annex III point 1(c) (high-risk from 2 Dec 2027) and Art. 50(3) (notice now), not by the ban.

Recital 18 takes physical states out of the notion of emotion altogether: "pain or fatigue, including, for example, systems used in detecting the state of fatigue of professional pilots or drivers" ([Act p.5](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. Fatigue detection for a shuttle driver or a night cook is thus outside the ban by a different route from the "medical or safety" exception, and the two routes have different conditions; outputs labelled "stress", "engagement" or "alertness" are emotions or intentions and are not rescued [T + I].

The existing enforcement precedent is under the GDPR, not the AI Act. The Hungarian data protection authority fined Budapest Bank HUF 250 million (about EUR 653,000 to 675,675 depending on the source) for AI analysis of customer-service call recordings that assessed callers' emotional state and was also used to rate staff; the findings were an uninformative privacy notice, no adequate impact assessment and no valid legal basis ([CMS, Hungary](https://cms.law/en/hun/legal-updates/hungary-data-authority-issues-heavy-fine-for-the-use-of-ai-voice-recording-analysis)) [S]. That is the "guest-only voice analytics" fact pattern.

### Manipulation and exploitation: a backstop behind consumer law

Arts. 5(1)(a) and (b) ban manipulative or deceptive techniques, and the exploitation of vulnerabilities "due to their age, disability or a specific social or economic situation", where behaviour is materially distorted and "significant harm" is reasonably likely ([Act p.51](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. Recital 29 shelters "common and legitimate commercial practices... that comply with the applicable law" and says intent to harm is not required ([Act pp.8–9](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. Demand-based dynamic pricing is outside. Personalised pricing is not a trigger in itself; the harm threshold is rarely met by one room-night, and the live constraint is consumer and data protection law [I]. The exposed cases are pricing or offers keyed to old age, disability or distress (an engine that removes cheaper options for a stranded traveller), and casino hotels whose player analytics time offers to loss-chasing guests; the guidelines' examples on older people, predatory finance and addictive reward loops point the same way [G] ([Arthur Cox summary](https://www.arthurcox.com/knowledge/the-eu-commission-guidelines-on-prohibited-ai-practices/)). No summary showed the guidelines discussing dynamic pricing, scarcity prompts, loyalty schemes or any hospitality example, so every hotel verdict here is inference.

### Social scoring, crime-risk profiling and face databases: absent from the team's material

Art. 5(1)(c) bans AI evaluation of people "over a certain period of time based on their social behaviour or known, inferred or predicted personal or personality characteristics" where the score leads to detrimental treatment either (i) "in social contexts that are unrelated to the contexts in which the data was originally generated" or (ii) "unjustified or disproportionate to their social behaviour or its gravity"; recital 31 confirms it covers "public or private actors" ([Act p.51, p.9](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The enforcer's central point is textual: **limb (ii) has no cross-context requirement**, so "same property, same context" is not a safe harbour; one noise complaint leading to a permanent AI-scored flag is "disproportionate to... its gravity" [T + I]. A manual "do not rent" flag entered by a night manager is not an AI system. Spend-based loyalty tiers by published rules are favourable treatment and usually not AI. An AI-scored incident history used for a proportionate, time-limited deposit holds conditionally. A group-wide or cross-brand "problem guest" score, and any score fed by reviews, social media or nationality, does not [I]. Recital 31's shelter covers only "lawful evaluation practices... for a specific purpose", so a score built without GDPR transparency loses it [T + I]. The words "social scoring" and "blacklist" do not appear in the booklet, while the market now sells exactly this: one vendor launched a pan-European guest risk score for short-let stays built on "biometric checks, behavioural data and stay history" with a green, yellow or red output (trade press, search summary) ([Europe Real Estate](https://europe-re.com/safeguest-launches-guest-risk-scoring-tool-to-tackle-problem-short-let-stays-across-europe/74353)) [S].

Three further points are missing from the team's files. Art. 5(1)(e) bans systems that "create or expand facial recognition databases through the untargeted scraping of facial images from the internet or CCTV footage", so enrolling every lobby visitor from the hotel's own cameras to "recognise returning guests" is likely banned; opt-in enrolment of named guests is targeted and is not [T + I]. Art. 5(1)(d) bans predicting that a person will commit an offence "based solely on the profiling of a natural person" and is not limited to police; a "likely to steal" score for new hires built from nationality or residence touches the wording, while fraud screening on "suspicious transactions" is expressly outside (recital 42) [T + I]. Art. 5(1)(g) bans inferring race, religion, political opinion, union membership or sexual orientation from biometric data; age and sex are not on that closed list, so age estimation at a self-service bar is not banned ([Act pp.51–52, p.12](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. A hotel's own face recognition is not prohibited by Art. 5(1)(h), which covers law-enforcement use only; it is high-risk "in so far as [its] use is permitted under relevant Union or national law", and recital 39 itself notes that data protection authorities have already prohibited private remote identification [T]. The Dutch authority's EUR 30.5 million GDPR fine on Clearview AI for building a face database shows where that enforcement comes from ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/dutch-pa-fines-facial-recognition-company-gdpr-breach)) [S].

The two new bans that apply from 2 December 2026 target "nudification" and AI-generated child sexual abuse material; use is prohibited only "where the deployer uses the system for the purpose of generating" it, and Omnibus recital 12 puts accidental output and ordinary swimwear imagery outside ([Omnibus Art. 5(1)(ba), (bb), (1a), p.18; recital 12, p.5](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)) [T]. For hotels this is an acceptable-use policy item, not a headline risk.

## High-risk AI: an employer problem first, a biometrics problem second

### Which Annex III areas reach a hotel

Annex III was not amended by the Omnibus [T, fact-check]. Three of its eight areas are live for an ordinary hotel. **Point 4 (employment)** covers systems for "recruitment or selection... to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates" and systems "to make decisions affecting terms of work-related relationships, the promotion or termination of work-related contractual relationships, to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour or personal traits or characteristics or to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of persons in such relationships" ([Act Annex III, p.127](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. **Point 1 (biometrics)** covers remote biometric identification, biometric categorisation by "sensitive or protected attributes", and emotion recognition; it expressly excludes "biometric verification the sole purpose of which is to confirm that a specific natural person is the person he or she claims to be" [T]. **Point 5(b)** covers creditworthiness evaluation of natural persons "with the exception of AI systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud" [T]. Points 2, 5(a), 5(c), 6, 7 and 8 are out on their wording (critical infrastructure supply, public benefits, life and health insurance pricing, law enforcement, migration, justice). Room, menu and table pricing is not on the list at all.

Two points are less settled than the team's data file says. Point 5(d), on systems "intended to evaluate and classify emergency calls", is not limited to public authorities, unlike point 5(a); an AI switchboard or panic-button system whose stated purpose includes prioritising emergency calls could fit its wording [T for the wording, fact-check; I for the hotel case]. Point 3 (education and vocational training) reaches hotel schools squarely and possibly accredited group academies; neither the team nor any researcher read the draft-guideline sections on points 2, 3, 5 or 6 to 8 [I].

In the employment area the line is drawn by what the provider says the tool is for: "intended purpose" is the use "intended by the provider... as specified in the information supplied by the provider in the instructions for use, promotional or sales materials and statements, as well as in the technical documentation" ([Act Art. 3(12), p.47](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. On the text alone, CV screening and ranking, video-interview scoring, seasonal-staff matching, targeted job advertising, rota or shift bidding ranked on reliability or ratings, per-attendant productivity scoring, dashboards ranking individual staff, and AI-set tips or bonuses are in [T + I]. The draft guidelines, as reported by the team's data file, both widen and narrow this [D]. Widening: scope covers freelancers, agency and platform workers; a human who "significantly relies" on the output is still a decision; "terms" include leave refusals and significant changes to regular hours; non-renewal of a fixed-term contract counts as termination; punctuality and reliability metrics are "personal traits"; monitoring stands alone without evaluation. Narrowing: allocation on stated availability, required accreditation or proximity is outside the task-allocation limb; break timing inside a shift is below a significance threshold; tools used exclusively for a legal duty, safety or property protection are out unless also used to evaluate staff; feedback that reaches only the worker is out. Both sets come from the same non-binding draft, and the hotel-counsel reviewer is right that the notes and the booklet treat the widening half as the working position and the narrowing half as "do not bank"; the honest wording is "the text says X; the draft reads it wider here and narrower there" [I].

**Purpose drift is the main hotel risk** [I, high-risk note]. Most of the booklet's "high-risk if" entries are operational tools (food-waste cameras, room inspection, stock-loss alerts, guest-review analytics, BI dashboards) that become employment systems when a manager starts using the output to judge a named person. Market evidence gathered by the coverage audit shows the decisive feature is standard, not rare: several housekeeping platforms advertise per-attendant "minutes per room" or "rooms cleaned per housekeeper" reports (vendor help pages, search summaries) ([Amadeus](https://help.amadeus-hospitality.com/operations/service-optimization/content/hskp-attendant-productivity-dashboard.html); [Flexkeeping](https://help.flexkeeping.com/en/support/solutions/articles/103000389938-new-analytics-dashboard)) [S].

### The Art. 6(3) filter belongs to the vendor and dies on profiling

An Annex III system is not high-risk where it "does not pose a significant risk of harm", which applies where it performs a narrow procedural task, improves a completed human activity, detects decision patterns, or does a preparatory task; but it "shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons" ([Act Art. 6(3), p.54](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The provider must document the assessment before placing on the market and register the system in the public EU database (Arts. 6(4), 49(2)); the Omnibus kept registration and slimmed its content [T]. A hotel that is only a deployer cannot invoke the filter itself. It is plausibly usable for a CV parser that only extracts fields and for interview scheduling, and unusable for anything that scores, ranks or flags individuals [I]. One correction to the research notes: the high-risk note said "an unregistered claim is not a valid one"; the hotel-counsel reviewer showed that the text does not say this. The filter is an objective test; documentation and registration are separate provider duties; and Art. 80 directs the authority's correction at "the relevant provider" ([Act Art. 80, p.107](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The practical advice is unchanged: ask the vendor for the registration reference.

### The product route after the Omnibus: thin for hotels

AI is high-risk on the product route only if it is a safety component of (or is itself) a product under Annex I law **and** that product must undergo third-party conformity assessment ([Act Art. 6(1), p.53](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. The Omnibus narrowed this in three ways. AI "solely used for non-safety related aspects of user assistance, performance optimisation, service efficiency, automation or convenience or quality control" is not a safety component (Art. 6(1a)), unless its "failure or malfunctioning... would endanger health and safety" (Art. 6(1b)); products needing third-party assessment only for radio-spectrum reasons do not count (Art. 6(1c)); and **machinery moved from Annex I Section A to Section B**, where "only Article 6(1), Article 60a and Articles 102 to 112 shall apply" ([Omnibus Art. 6(1a)–(1c), p.18; Art. 2(2), p.15; Annex I change, p.36](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)) [T, confirmed by the fact-checker].

The practical consequence, which the booklet does not state, is that a hotel using kitchen robots, cleaning and delivery robots or automatic doors owes **no Art. 26 deployer duties for the safety AI inside that machinery**; AI requirements reach the maker through the Machinery Regulation by 2 August 2028 [T + I]. The enforcer's limit is equally textual: Art. 2(2) shields "AI systems classified as high-risk... in accordance with Article 6(1)", not the machine. The robot's voice interface still falls under Art. 50(1), and a camera feed used to score how fast cooks work is Annex III point 4(b) [I]. Lifts, gas appliances, pressure equipment, toys and medical devices stay in Section A; there the maker is the provider and the hotel is a deployer with Art. 26 duties from 2 August 2028 [T + I]. The team's "lifts, gas appliances, pressure equipment" summary omits medical devices in spas (AI skin or body analysers, if they are medical devices needing a notified body), cableways and recreational craft at resorts [I]. The words "predictive maintenance" appear nowhere in Art. 6 or recital 7; whether fault prediction for a lift is "performance optimisation" is a reading that turns on the "solely" and "would endanger" tests [T, fact-check: "partly right"].

### What a hotel owes as user of a high-risk tool from 2 December 2027

| Duty | Provision | Limits written into the text |
|---|---|---|

| Assign human oversight to people with "competence, training and authority" | Art. 26(2) | The hotel keeps "freedom to organise its own resources" (Art. 26(3)); the people need not be employees |

| Monitor; inform maker and authority and suspend use where there is a risk; report serious incidents | Art. 26(5) | Suspension is tied to a "risk within the meaning of Article 79(1)"; a discriminatory outcome is a "serious incident" (Art. 3(49)(c)) |
| Keep automatically generated logs at least six months | Art. 26(6) | Only logs "under their control"; subject to data protection law |
| Before use at work, inform workers' representatives and affected workers | Art. 26(7) | Information only; addressed to "deployers who are employers" |
| Tell people subject to an Annex III system that makes or assists decisions about them | Art. 26(11) | Annex III only; this, not 26(7), covers candidates and guests |
| Cooperate with authorities | Art. 26(12) | — |
| Explain, on request, the role of the AI and the main elements of a decision | Art. 86 | Only decisions with legal or similarly significant effect that the person considers adverse; yields to other Union law |

Source: [Act Arts. 26, 86, pp.67–69, 110](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) [T]; the split between 26(7) and 26(11) was confirmed by the fact-checker. Art. 26 breaches carry fines up to EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover (Art. 99(4)(e)); Arts. 27 and 86 are not on that list [T]. A private hotel owes **no fundamental-rights impact assessment** for employment or biometric tools (Art. 27(1) covers public bodies, private entities providing public services, and credit and insurance scoring) and **does not register** as deployer (Art. 49(3) covers public authorities) [T]. The enforcer adds a real caveat: a hotel contracted by a state or municipality to house asylum seekers or evacuees is arguably a "private entit[y] providing public services", and a hotel school is one for education [I]. These duties cannot be transferred to the vendor, but the hotel-counsel reviewer is right that performance can be delegated: Art. 26(2) asks for competent "natural persons", not employees, so a management company or contractor can carry out oversight and log-keeping while the deployer stays answerable [T + I].

### Becoming the maker: four routes, one of them unsettled

A hotel group becomes provider by building a tool and putting it into service under its own name (Art. 3(3)), or, for high-risk systems, by putting its name on one, substantially modifying one, or modifying the intended purpose of a non-high-risk system "in such a way that the AI system concerned becomes a high-risk AI system" ([Act Art. 25(1), p.67](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. A provider of an employment tool owes the full package of Arts. 9 to 15, a quality management system, internal-control conformity assessment, CE marking and registration; for a biometric tool (Annex III point 1) a notified body is required unless harmonised standards or common specifications are applied in full (Art. 43(1)) [T, fact-check]. The simplified quality-management route in Art. 63(1) is open to SMEs only where "they do not have partner enterprises or linked enterprises", which excludes most group-owned hotel companies [T, fact-check].

The route the team calls "most common in hospitality", a manager ranking CVs in a general chatbot, is the least settled, and three researchers and reviewers end in the same place. On the text, intended purpose is set by the provider, and off-label use by a user is "reasonably foreseeable misuse" (Art. 3(13)), a concept the Act uses for the provider's risk management, not as a role switch; Art. 25(1)(c) needs someone who "modif[ies] the intended purpose". A one-off prompt modifies nothing; a standing workflow that the hotel configures and wires into its applicant system looks like putting a system into service for own use [T + I]. On the draft guidelines, a general system whose vendor does not consistently exclude high-risk uses is deemed intended for them, so the vendor is the high-risk provider and the hotel a deployer [D]. Where the vendor does consistently forbid HR use, the hotel that ignores it becomes provider and Art. 25(2) removes the vendor's duty to cooperate, leaving a system the hotel cannot make compliant; the only lawful course is to stop [T + I]. None of this bites before 2 December 2027. Today the constraint on that manager is the GDPR.

## Transparency, literacy and enforcement: the duty map differs from the common summary

### Who owes the "you are talking to AI" notice

Art. 50(1) reads "Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural persons concerned are informed", unless this is "obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect" ([Act p.82](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. A hotel that only deploys a vendor's bot has no duty under that paragraph, and the transparency note observes that a fine on a pure deployer for a missing chatbot notice "has no clear legal basis" [T + I]. Both reviewers accept the text and disagree on how often it helps. The enforcer argues that many "hotel bots" are the hotel's own system: a concierge assembled on a model API or a no-code builder, with the hotel's own instructions, knowledge base and booking connection, run under the hotel's name, makes the hotel a provider under Art. 3(3) and a "downstream provider" under Art. 3(68) with no need for Art. 25 [T + I]. Choosing a name, logo and FAQ content in a finished product does not; where exactly configuration becomes "developing" is open. A hotel that switches a vendor's notice off, or lets a bot pose as a person, creates a misleading practice under consumer law whoever owes the AI Act notice [I, from memory]. The restrictive reading of "obvious" (customer chatbots, realistic avatars and human-like voices never qualify; first-turn disclosure; spoken statement at call start; a tone alone is insufficient) is the Commission's, from guidelines no researcher could open [G]; the Act's own test is contextual.

### What falls on the hotel directly: emotion and biometric notices, deep fakes, public-interest text

Art. 50(3) obliges "Deployers of an emotion recognition system or a biometric categorisation system" to inform the people exposed; it has applied since 2 August 2026 whatever the tool's risk tier [T, fact-check]. It reaches voice analytics scoring callers' mood, cameras estimating guest mood, age or gender, and facial age estimation at a self-service alcohol point, because a "biometric categorisation system" is any system assigning people to categories from biometric data unless "ancillary to another commercial service and strictly necessary for objective technical reasons" (Art. 3(40)) [T + I]. A notice is not GDPR consent.

Art. 50(4) obliges deployers to disclose deep fakes, defined as AI-generated or manipulated image, audio or video that "resembles existing persons, objects, places, entities or events and would falsely appear to a person to be authentic or truthful" (Art. 3(60)) ([Act pp.50, 82](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. On that definition a realistic AI picture of the actual hotel that differs from reality, a cloned staff voice on the phone menu, an avatar of the general manager and a lip-synced translated video are all caught; colour correction and cropping are not; a fantastical image is not [I]. Consent of the person depicted is no exception, and a label does not cure misleading advertising [I]. The booklet's test, "realistic AI images... if they could mislead", is narrower than the Act in two ways: audio and video are covered, and the test is resemblance plus false authenticity. The text limb covers AI text "published with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest", except where it "has undergone a process of human review or editorial control and where a natural or legal person holds editorial responsibility" [T]. That marketing copy is outside while health, consumer-safety and sustainability claims are inside is the guidelines' reading [G]. The transparency note required "a named person, real fact-checking and a record"; the hotel-counsel reviewer correctly notes these are not in the text, which allows the hotel company itself to hold editorial responsibility. A record is simply the only practical proof.

The legacy rule does not rescue older chatbots. The scope note pointed out that Art. 111(2), read literally, says "this Regulation shall apply... only if" a legacy high-risk system changes, which would switch off Arts. 4 and 50 for it; the fact-checker called this "a fair reading of the words" that "the text does not resolve". Both reviewers reject it for the same reason: Art. 111(4) gives Art. 50(2) its own narrow transition, which would be pointless if legacy systems were outside Art. 50 [T + I]. That transition covers only generators "placed on the market" before 2 August 2026; an in-house generator that was only "put into service" is not named, so its marking duty arguably began on 2 August 2026 [T for the wording; I for the consequence].

### AI literacy: softened, still binding, no EU-tier fine

Art. 4 now reads "shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf", and adds that it "does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual" ([Omnibus p.16](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)) [T]. The old text required measures "to ensure, to their best extent, a sufficient level". Art. 4 is not in the Art. 99(4) fine list [T, fact-check]. The enforcer's three levers against "no fine, so optional" are sound: the Omnibus changed Art. 99(1) from "infringements" to "any infringement", so Member States must have some measure for it; from 2 December 2027 untrained oversight staff are a direct, fined breach of Art. 26(2); and absence of training shows negligence under Art. 99(7) in any other fine [T + I]. Germany's implementing law reportedly attaches no fine to Art. 4 ([ki-syndikat.de](https://www.ki-syndikat.de/blog/202609/ki-schulung-pflicht-kein-bussgeld-haftung/)) [S]. The softer duty is easier to meet and easier to breach visibly: doing nothing is now clearly non-compliant.

Source: [Act Art. 99(3)–(6), pp.115–116](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [Omnibus Art. 99(1), (4)(da), (6a), p.35](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj) [T, confirmed by the fact-checker]. The transparency note's worked ceilings for an Art. 50 breach are arithmetic on these rules, not observed fines: an independent hotel company with EUR 8 million turnover that is an SME faces at most 3% = EUR 240,000; a small mid-cap group with EUR 120 million turnover at most EUR 3.6 million; a group with EUR 5 billion turnover up to EUR 150 million [I]. "Undertaking" is undefined in the Act; reviewers and commentators expect the competition-law meaning (the economic unit, so group turnover), by analogy with GDPR fines, which is untested under the AI Act [I, from memory; S ([activemind.legal](https://www.activemind.legal/legislation/ai-act/article-99/))]. SME status is defined by reference to Recommendation 2003/361/EC and small mid-cap status to Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099, reported as fewer than 750 staff and turnover up to EUR 150 million or balance sheet up to EUR 129 million ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/30473-european-commission-adopted-recommendation-eu-20251099-on-small-mid-cap-enterprises)) [S]; whether a property company owned by a fund counts as an SME on stand-alone figures is unresolved, and the safe assumption is that it does not.

Fines need a national law and a designated authority. The picture in November 2026, from search summaries and the team's data file, none read in the original: Germany (Bundesnetzagentur; implementing law in force 29 July 2026, reportedly with national fines up to EUR 50,000 for Arts. 21, 27, 45 and 86), Italy (ACN; Legislative Decree 179/2026 in force 23 October 2026) and Greece (the data protection authority; Law 5321/2026) have rules in force; France, Spain and Austria reportedly do not yet; Sweden runs an interim mandate ([HLC on Germany](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/eu-ai-act-german-implementation-act-enters-into-force); [Cleary on Italy](https://www.clearygottlieb.com/news-and-insights/publication-listing/italy-ai-regulation-new-rules-and-risks-for-businesses); [EY on Greece](https://www.ey.com/en_gr/technical/tax/tax-alerts/ai-act-greek-legal-framework-for-the-supervision-of-artificial-intelligence-systems)) [S]. A missing national law delays the AI Act fine, not the breach. No confirmed AI Act fine against any operator was found as of November 2026; one tracker records that every fine so far was imposed under existing law, chiefly the GDPR ([vorplabs.com](https://vorplabs.com/ai-regulatory-updates/eu-enforcement)) [S]. Absence in searches is not proof of absence.

The realistic order of pressure on a hotel in 2026 to 2028 is therefore: a data protection authority after a guest or staff complaint; a works council or union under national labour law; competitors and trade or consumer associations under unfair-competition law (German commentary treats Art. 50 as a probable market-conduct rule, [Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/de/news-events/newsletter-fachbeitraege/artikel/wer-kennzeichnen-muss-und-welche-risiken-drohen.html)) [S]; a consumer authority; and only then a market surveillance authority acting on an Art. 85 complaint [I]. The Act also plugs into two channels the team's files never mention: collective consumer actions (Art. 110 adds the Act to the Representative Actions Directive) and whistleblower protection (Art. 87) ([Act pp.110, 121](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. Chapter V on general-purpose models binds model makers, not hotels; the assistants hotels buy from the large model makers are supervised by the AI Office, not by national authorities (Art. 75(1)) [T].

Art. 99(7) turns preparation into a legal factor: an authority must weigh "the technical and organisational measures implemented", cooperation, self-reporting, "the intentional or negligent character of the infringement" and mitigation "when deciding whether to impose an administrative fine" ([Act p.116](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)) [T]. This is the legal reason for the tool list, the vendor file and the training record. A file that describes a configuration the tool never had is worse than none, because Art. 99(5) fines misleading information.

## The 91-use catalogue: labels live only in prose, two counts do not reproduce, and about twenty uses are missing

### What the booklet's headline counts rest on

The catalogue holds 91 uses in 15 departments (with 152 vendors and 41 hotel groups) and none of the team's three data files stores an AI Act label per use; the label exists only as prose in the booklet's A6 table and Part C tables ([catalogue](../tools/use-case-catalogue/index.html); [coverage audit, section 1](../research_notes/EU%20AI%20Act%20hospitality%20deep%20dive/hotel_use_case_coverage_audit.md)). The fact-checker reproduced the counts 3, 13 and 11 from the A6 table as extracted from the PDF text. Two problems remain. **Two of the 13 are lift-door AI and hotel-run gas or pressure safety AI, while the two catalogue uses they stand for (positions 51 and 52) are predictive maintenance**, a different function that the catalogue itself marks "announced only" for the hotel-run case; on a strict match the figure is 11. ### One feature changes the label

Seventeen single changes move a tool up a label and nine move it down, and almost all are a reporting setting, a data source or a use of the output, not a different product [I, coverage audit]. The up-switches that matter most: switching on "sentiment from voice" for guests (transparency to high-risk plus Art. 50(3)); the same for staff (to prohibited); adding punctuality or ratings as rota inputs; reporting minutes per room per attendant; attributing camera, waste or stock results to a named person; pointing an ad tool at job advertisements; letting AI send replies itself instead of a person reviewing and sending. A manager's sign-off, a terms-of-service line and a short retention period are not down-switches under the Act; retention is a GDPR measure. Whether switching a monitoring feature off declassifies a tool is disputed and is treated in the next section.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the advice more than its direction. The booklet's instinct, that hotels are exposed as employers, around biometrics, when telling guests, and as accidental makers, survives every challenge; what does not survive is the single voice in which it speaks. A hotel reader needs three registers kept visibly apart: what the regulation obliges today (four duties for a vendor-tool hotel), what another law already obliges (most of what the booklet's "do now" list contains), and what is simply prudent because Art. 99(7) rewards it. The same separation applies to sources. The points on which hotels will most want certainty, staff text analytics, guest-only emotion tools, availability-only rostering, later purchases of a legacy model and white-label clauses, all rest on a recital, an unopened guideline or a draft, and the report should say so beside each claim.

The second shift is in where the risk sits. No AI Act fine against anyone was found by November 2026, the high-risk regime is a year away, and several focus countries reportedly have no penalty law; yet almost every exit that holds under the Act ends in the column "caught anyway by". For HFTP's members the practical reading is that the AI Act is the organising frame and the calendar, while data protection authorities, works councils, competitors and consumer bodies are the enforcers for the next two years. Two planning decisions follow that the team's material does not yet pose: fixing, with dated evidence, the legacy position of every staff-facing tool before 2 December 2027, and deciding for every guest-facing bot whether the hotel assembled it, in which case the hotel, not the vendor, owes the notice. And one opening exists for the client itself: Arts. 95 and 96 let a sector body draw up a deployer code of conduct and feed hotel evidence into the pending Commission guidelines on Arts. 25 and 26 and on "substantial modification", which is where the open rows of the register will be decided.

This report is research for compliance planning. It is not legal advice, and no exit described here has been tested before an authority or a court.
