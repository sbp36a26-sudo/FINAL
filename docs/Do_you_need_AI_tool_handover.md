## 1. What the tool is

**"Do you need AI?"** is one tab of a web tool hub (a claude.ai artifact) built for the project. It is a guided self-check for **hotel managers who know nothing about AI**: general managers, owners, department heads, IT and finance people in European hotels. They think of **one problem** in their hotel (for example "we answer the same guest emails all day") and the tool answers three questions in order:

1. **Is this an AI problem at all?** Five yes/no questions. If fixed rules can do the job, the tool says "This is not suitable for AI." and stops.
2. **Are the basics in place?** Ten "must-haves" that any AI project needs (an owner with time, a first-year budget, an approved KPI, systems that can connect, a data protection contact, someone who checks the AI, data you can reach, staff told, rules for staff on AI tools, a way to work when the tool is down).
3. **What does this particular use case need?** The hotel picks one of **99 use cases** (from the team's Use-Case Catalogue); the tool then asks 6 to 9 tailored questions about people, skills, money, technology, data and organisation for that use.

It ends with a **verdict** (one of four) and a **score in %**, a short action plan by person, questions to ask vendors, a provisional EU AI Act label for the use case, and a PDF.

**Why it exists:** hotels are offered AI tools before anyone has checked whether AI is the right answer. The cover quotes the reason: among EU accommodation businesses with 10 or more staff that had considered AI but did not use it, the most common reasons were a lack of expertise (70%) and systems that do not fit together (46%). Source: Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2).

**What it is not:** it does not check anyone's answers (they are the manager's own), it is not legal advice, and its cut-offs are the team's judgement, untested with hotels. It is a student prototype by EHL SBP Group 36 for HFTP. It has **not yet been tested with real managers**; a timed test with 3 to 5 managers is planned (session kit: `reports/Do you need AI - session kit.md`).

**Project rules the tool follows** (from `CLAUDE.md`): every number or statistic carries its source and base next to it; no Wikipedia; no material from an organisation that competes with HFTP is cited (see `CLAUDE.md`); bird-themed, not AI-looking design under `brand/brand.json`; plain words for non-experts.

## 3. How it is set up

### 3.1 Technology

Plain HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript, no framework. One self-contained page (`tools/readiness-2/index.html`, about 1.2 MB) is generated by `node tools/readiness-2/build.mjs` from the sources below, then embedded in the hub as a frame. Everything is inlined (the claude.ai artifact policy blocks other hosts): the catalogue data, the generated questions, a bundled PDF library (jsPDF) and one font. The hub page `tools/index.html` is generated by `node tools/build-hub.mjs` and must never be edited by hand.

- **Use cases and sources:** the Use-Case Catalogue tab (`tools/use-case-catalogue/index.html`, 99 uses in 15 departments, each with its task, AI technique, hotel-adoption rating High/Medium/Low and source list). The tool bundles what it needs at build time.

- **The provisional EU AI Act label:** the team's AI Register (`tools/register/`); marked "provisional, awaiting legal sign-off, not legal advice".

### 3.4 Running inside the hub

The hub (`tools/index.html`) shows each tool in an iframe and talks to it through `postMessage` (contract: `tools/hub/CONTRACTS.md`): `ping/pong` (is a hub present), `download` (the claude.ai viewer blocks downloads from frames, so the PDF is handed to the hub to save), `db` (team sharing, currently off), `open` (jump to another tab, `#use=<id>` preselects a use case), and `detail` / `detail-close` / `detail-closed` (the hub draws the "Details and sources" layer on its main page because **links inside a tool frame do not open in the claude.ai viewer, while links on the hub's own page do**). Frames load as `<tool>/index.html?v=<content hash>` so a publish is never shown stale.

### 3.5 Storage and sharing

- Everything stays **in the visitor's browser** (`localStorage` key `hftp-readiness2-v1`, store version 3). Several checks can be kept; the open one is remembered per tab. "Save as file" / "Open a saved file" move a check between browsers (`{kind: "hftp-do-you-need-ai", v: 3, rules, savedAt, check}`).
- Records saved by older versions open and are migrated once (for example the v2.6 turn-round of question 1; old "Partly" and "Doesn't apply" answers show as unanswered with a "please choose again" line).
- **Team sharing** (a copy to the hub's database, shown on the red Team tab) is built but **switched off** (`SHARING_DEFAULT = false` in `src/app.js`) until the data notice is complete.

## 4. The flow, screen by screen

Screens in order: **Cover → 1 About your hotel → 2 Is this an AI problem? → (Stop) → 3 Must-haves → 4 Where in the hotel? → 5 Questions for this use case → Result.** A progress bar of five segments runs along the top; "Start a new check" is always available except on the stop and result screens (on the result it sits beside Download (PDF)).

### 4.0 Cover (state `start`)

Three panels, then **Start**. If a use case was preselected through a link (`#use=<id>`), a line "You came here for: <name>. It is already chosen in step 4." comes first.

- **Why this tool:** "Hotels are offered more and more AI tools, often before anyone has checked whether AI is the right answer to the problem. This tool helps you check that first, before you spend time and money with vendors: does the job need AI at all, are the basics in place, and what does this particular use need?" plus the Eurostat line from section 1.
- **How to use it:** "Think of **one problem** in your hotel, for example “we answer the same guest emails all day”. Then go through five steps:" About your hotel · Is this an AI problem? (five short questions; if fixed rules can do the job the tool says so and stops) · Must-haves · Where in the hotel? (among 99 use cases in 15 departments) · Questions for this use case (6 to 9 questions on people, skills, money, technology, data and rules). Then: "Answer honestly; “Don’t know yet” is a fine answer and goes on your to-do list. Each question has a “Why this matters” link if you want more. You can stop and come back: your answers stay in this browser."
- **What you get:** a clear answer (AI, simple rules, or not yet); a verdict and a score; what is missing (three things to start with, an action plan by person you can copy); questions for the vendor and the provisional EU AI Act label; a PDF and a file to share and to come back to.
- The honesty line: "Nobody checks your answers. An answer that isn’t true only hides a problem you will meet later."
- Also on the cover: **Your checks in this browser** (list of saved checks) and **Open a saved file**.

### 4.2 Step 2: "Is this an AI problem?" (state `q`)

Heading shows the acronym **C-R-I-S-P** (Complex judgement, Return, Information to learn from, Some mistakes are acceptable, Proof). Intro: "Think of one problem in your hotel. Five questions: a No to question 1 and a Yes to the others point towards AI. If you are not sure, say so: it becomes something to find out." Each question is answered **Yes / No / Not sure** and has a "Why this matters" fold (one-line reason, "Answer Yes if" and "Answer No if" boxes with examples).

**1. Complex judgement** (Needs judgement, not fixed rules)

- Question: **Can each case be fixed with an “if this, then that” rule?**
- Hint: Yes if you could write it down as fixed rules, like “send an email 3 days before arrival” or “reorder towels when stock falls below 200”. No if every case is different, like guest emails, photos or tomorrow’s demand.
- Why this matters: AI only helps when a job needs judgement. If fixed rules can do the job, ordinary software is cheaper and more reliable.
- Answer Yes if: You could write it down as fixed rules, like “send an email 3 days before arrival” or “reorder towels when stock falls below 200”. Example: Sending a pre-arrival email three days before arrival, or alerting when a room is not cleaned by 14:00.
- Answer No if: Every case is different, like guest emails, photos or tomorrow’s demand. Example: Reading guest emails in five languages and drafting replies.
- Not sure?: Think about the job itself, not how you do it today. If you use rules now only because nothing better existed (for example price rules), answer Not sure.
- If this answer decides the outcome: "Not suitable for AI: rules or ordinary automation will do it." (who finds out: the vendor or IT)

**2. Return** (Worth real money or hours)

- Question: **Is this task frequent or costly enough to be worth improving?**
- Why this matters: AI costs money to set up, run and check, so a rare or small job will not pay that back.
- Answer Yes if: It happens often or costs a lot, for example many cases a week, or many staff hours or much revenue involved. Example: About 400 guest emails a week at front office.
- Answer No if: It is rare or small, so doing it better would save little. Example: A 12-room guesthouse with 10 email enquiries a week.
- If this answer decides the outcome: "Probably not worth it." Advice: Look again if the volume grows, or if a tool you already pay for adds it. (who finds out: the GM or finance)

**3. Information to learn from** (Many past examples to learn from)

- Question: **Are there many past examples of this task, such as emails, bookings or photos, that show what a good result looks like?**
- Why this matters: AI learns from examples; without many past cases it has nothing to learn from.
- Answer Yes if: You have hundreds of past cases stored digitally. Example: Two years of guest emails and the replies staff sent, kept in the mailbox.
- Answer No if: It happens rarely, or nothing was ever recorded. Example: A special request that comes up a few times a year and was never written down.
- If this answer decides the outcome: "Too few examples to learn from." Advice: Start keeping these cases digitally, or ask the vendor what examples its tool learned from. Then ask again. (who finds out: IT or the department head)

**4. Some mistakes are acceptable** (Some mistakes are acceptable)

- Question: **Is it acceptable if the result is right most of the time, but not every time?**
- Why this matters: AI is never right every time; if no mistake is acceptable, a rule-based tool or a person is the better choice.
- Answer Yes if: A wrong answer now and then can be caught or does little harm. Example: Draft replies to reviews that a manager approves before they go out.
- Answer No if: Every single result must be exactly right, for example a legal or safety decision. Example: Answering guests’ allergen questions on its own.
- If this answer decides the outcome: "A rule-based tool or a person is the better choice." Advice: If the use touches safety, health or people’s rights (hiring, staff evaluation), check the AI Act guide tab: it may be high-risk. (who finds out: the department head)

**5. Proof** (Success has a number)

- Question: **Do you know which KPI should improve, and what it is today?**
- Hint: Examples: reply time, hours spent, revenue per room, kilos of food thrown away.
- Why this matters: Without today’s figure you cannot tell later whether the tool worked, so keeping it becomes guesswork.
- Answer Yes if: You can name one number and know today’s figure, such as reply time, hours spent or revenue per room. Example: “Average reply time to email enquiries: 9 hours today; target under 1 hour.”
- Answer No if: You only have a general aim, like “be more innovative”. Example: “We want to be more innovative.”
- If this answer decides the outcome: "Define success first." Advice: Write down the number and today’s figure, then come back. (who finds out: the GM)

**Outcome of step 2** (rules in section 5.1): Q1 **Yes** stops the check. Otherwise the headline is "Probably not worth it." (Q2 No), "Too few examples to learn from." (Q3 No), "A rule-based tool or a person is the better choice." (Q4 No), "Define success first." (Q5 No), "Find out first." (any Not sure), or "Worth exploring with AI. Next: the must-haves." A No on Q2 to Q5 does not stop the check: it is carried to the result and holds back the top verdict.

**Stop screen** (state `stop`, Q1 = Yes): headline "This is not suitable for AI."; "The job follows rules a person can write down, so rules or ordinary automation will do it, and a simpler tool is cheaper, easier to explain and easier to fix."; "Instead: set up a rule, a report or an automatic message in the software you already have, or change the process. Ask your current software vendor whether a rule or report can do it."; "If a vendor offers you an AI tool for this job, ask what the AI does that a rule could not. If you are unsure, change your answer to Not sure: it becomes something to find out." Buttons: **Change my answers**, **Start again**, **Download (PDF)** (a one-page PDF). Nothing past the stop is saved or counted.

### 4.3 Step 3: Must-haves (state `must`)

Ten things to have in place before an AI project starts. Think of the project you have in mind. Under each one you can see how many of the 90 use cases in the team’s analysis need it. All ten need an answer. Answers: **Yes / Planned, not done yet / No / Don't know yet**. "Planned" offers an optional **By when?** date. A collapsed "Why this matters" shows the reason, what counts as Yes, the share of use cases that need it, and sourced figures with base and date. The ten:

**1. A named owner with time** (owner of the task: General manager)

- Question: **Is there a named person in charge of this AI project, with agreed hours each week for it?**
- Why: Without one person who owns it, nobody watches the results, deals with the vendor or decides on changes.
- What counts as Yes: A named role with weekly hours written into a job description or the project plan.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Name the person in charge of this AI project and agree the hours they will spend on it.
- Evidence: “Roles and responsibilities and lines of communication related to mapping, measuring, and managing AI risks are documented and are clear to individuals and teams throughout the organization.” *NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, GOVERN 2.1 (Jan 2023).*

**2. Budget for the full first year** (owner of the task: Finance)

- Question: **Is there a written budget for the first 12 months that covers the full cost, not only the subscription?**
- Why: AI keeps costing money after launch: the subscription, fees based on use, connecting it to your systems, training, and staff time to check results.
- What counts as Yes: A budget line covering subscription, set-up and connection to your systems, training and running costs.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Write a 12-month budget line for the full cost (subscription, set-up, connection, training, running costs).
- Evidence: 41% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it said the costs seem too high. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*
- Evidence: 35% of hotels named high set-up costs as a barrier. *HES-SO Valais-Wallis survey, news of 9 Jul 2025. Base: over 1,500 hotels in Austria, France, Germany, Greece, Italy and Switzerland, Jan–Apr 2025.*

**3. A number to improve, approved** (owner of the task: Owner or head office)

- Question: **Has the person who approves spending agreed the number (KPI) this tool must improve, with today’s figure and the target?**
- Why: Without today’s figure and a target, nobody can tell later whether the tool worked, and keeping it becomes a matter of opinion.
- What counts as Yes: The number (KPI), today’s figure and the target, approved in writing and dated.
- Explanation shown: KPI: the one number that shows success, for example reply time or revenue per room. In a managed or franchised hotel the owner approves it.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Agree the KPI this tool must improve, today’s figure and the target, and get them approved by the person who decides on spending.
- Evidence: “Lack of senior management buy-in in many companies also appears problematic.” *OECD, The Adoption of Artificial Intelligence in Firms, May 2025. Base: survey of 840 enterprises in G7 countries, 2022–23, plus interviews.*
- Evidence: Only 14% of hotel company leaders credited executive mandates for their AI success, against 34% for experimentation: light written backing, not a top-down order. *State of Hotel AI, Fall 2026, commissioned by Destination AI (research by Josiah Mackenzie, Actabl). Base: 107 hotel company leaders, worldwide, Aug–Sep 2026; global survey, vendor-linked; not comparable with the European figures.*

**4. Systems that can connect** (owner of the task: IT)

- Question: **Can new AI tools easily be plugged in to your existing infrastructure?**
- Why: An AI tool that cannot read from and write back to your hotel systems leaves staff copying data by hand.
- What counts as Yes: For each system you want to connect, its provider has confirmed that other software can connect to the version you use.
- Explanation shown: PMS: the property management system, the booking system that holds reservations, rooms and guest records.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Ask the provider of each system you want to connect (for example (examples of the hotel's systems for the chosen department, e.g. PMS, POS)) whether other software can read from it and write back to it.
- Evidence: 46% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it named incompatibility with their existing equipment, software or systems as a reason. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*
- Evidence: 29% of hotel company leaders named systems integration as a barrier to getting value from AI. *State of Hotel AI, Fall 2026, commissioned by Destination AI (research by Josiah Mackenzie, Actabl). Base: 107 hotel company leaders, worldwide, Aug–Sep 2026; global survey, vendor-linked; not comparable with the European figures.*

**5. Data protection contact involved** (owner of the task: Data protection contact)

- Question: **Has your data protection contact been told about this plan?**
- Why: If the tool handles guest or staff data, data protection law asks for a written contract with the vendor and sometimes a written risk check. Your data protection contact knows what applies.
- What counts as Yes: Your data protection contact knows which guest or staff data the tool would use and has said what is needed before you start.
- Explanation shown: If you have no data protection officer, this is whoever handles data protection for you, often an outside adviser.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Tell your data protection contact about the plan and ask what is needed before any guest or staff data goes into a tool.
- Evidence: 52% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it named concerns about data protection and privacy. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*
- Evidence: “Such legal act must be in writing, including in electronic form.” (on the contract between a hotel and a vendor that processes its personal data) *EDPB Guidelines 07/2020 on the concepts of controller and processor, version 2.1.*

**6. A person who checks the AI** (owner of the task: Head of the department using it)

- Question: **Is there a named person who will check the AI’s results and can overrule them?**
- Why: AI is never right every time. Someone must be able to catch a wrong result and put it right before it is used.
- What counts as Yes: A named role that checks the results, knows which results must be checked before they are used, and can stop or correct the tool.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Name the person who checks the AI’s results, and decide which results they must check before they count.
- Evidence: 57% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it named a lack of clarity about the legal consequences. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*

**7. Data you can reach and keep correct** (owner of the task: Head of the department using it)

- Question: **Is each piece of data this tool needs held in a system, with a named person who keeps it correct?**
- Why: AI works from your data. If the facts are on paper, out of date or spread over systems that disagree, the tool gives wrong answers that sound sure.
- What counts as Yes: You can name where each piece of data sits, and a named person checks it at set times.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: List the data the tool needs and the system each piece sits in, and name who keeps it right.
- Evidence: 43% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it named difficulties with the availability or quality of the data. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*
- Evidence: 42% of hotel company leaders were confident their data is ready for their AI goals; 48% were not. *State of Hotel AI, Fall 2026, commissioned by Destination AI (research by Josiah Mackenzie, Actabl). Base: 107 hotel company leaders, worldwide, Aug–Sep 2026; global survey, vendor-linked; not comparable with the European figures.*

**8. Staff told, and able to report errors** (owner of the task: Head of the department using it)

- Question: **Have the staff involved been told about the plan, and do they know who to tell when the tool gets something wrong?**
- Why: Staff who were not told work around a tool, and errors nobody reports are never fixed.
- What counts as Yes: The staff involved have heard the plan from their manager, and one named person collects what goes wrong.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Tell the staff concerned about the plan, and name the person they tell when the tool gets something wrong.
- Evidence: 70% of EU accommodation businesses that had considered AI but did not use it named a lack of relevant expertise, the most common reason. *Eurostat, ICT usage survey, early 2025 (dataset isoc_eb_ain2). Base: EU accommodation enterprises with 10+ persons employed.*
- Evidence: 35% of hotel company leaders named change management as a barrier to getting value from AI, the most often cited. *State of Hotel AI, Fall 2026, commissioned by Destination AI (research by Josiah Mackenzie, Actabl). Base: 107 hotel company leaders, worldwide, Aug–Sep 2026; global survey, vendor-linked; not comparable with the European figures.*

**9. Rules for staff on AI tools** (owner of the task: General manager)

- Question: **Do you have written rules telling staff what they must not type into AI tools?**
- Why: Without a rule, guest or company data ends up in tools nobody has checked.
- What counts as Yes: One page every member of staff has seen, saying what must never go into an AI tool.
- Explanation shown: For example guest data, staff data or contracts.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Write one page of rules on what staff must not put into AI tools.
- Evidence: Only 9% of the hotels surveyed had a formal AI strategy document. *Britz & Schegg, From Awareness to Action: AI Readiness in Hotels, HES-SO Valais-Wallis, Oct 2025. Base: hoteliers surveyed Jul–Sep 2025, median 37.5 rooms; the authors warn the sample is likely more digitally aware than average.*

**10. A way to work when the tool is down** (owner of the task: Head of the department using it)

- Question: **If the tool stops working for a day, is there a written way to do the job without it?**
- Why: Tools fail and vendors change. The work still has to be done that day.
- What counts as Yes: A short written backup plan for each task the tool does, which the shift leaders know.
- If not Yes, the action plan says: Write down how the job gets done if the tool stops working, and walk the shift leaders through it.
- Evidence: “Contingency processes are in place to handle failures or incidents in third-party data or AI systems deemed to be high-risk.” *NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, GOVERN 6.2 (Jan 2023).*

## 5. The rules

### 5.1 Step 2 outcome (`verdict()` in `src/logic.js`, precedence Q1 > Q2 > Q3 > Q4 > Q5)

1. Q1 = Yes → **stop** ("This is not suitable for AI."). Nothing further is asked or saved.
2. Otherwise the headline is the first that applies: Q2 No → "worth" · Q3 No → "data" · Q4 No → "exact" · Q5 No → "success" · any Not sure → "find" · else "go". Every No on Q2 to Q5 is **carried** to the result. Every Not sure is listed as something to find out.

### 5.2 Answers and points (steps 3 and 5)

| Answer | Points | Counts as |
|---|---|---|
| Yes | 2 | in place |
| Planned, not done yet | 1 | planned (not done: it still holds a must-have back) |
| No | 0 | missing |
| Don't know yet | 0 | "to find out" (team judgement `UNKNOWN_SCORES_ZERO`) |
| (blank, or an old "Partly" / "Doesn't apply") | none | not answered; the tool asks again |

**Score** = points ÷ points possible over every must-have plus every tailored question asked (2 per question, "Don't know yet" included in the possible points), rounded **down**, shown as "Score: n%" with "x of y points on z questions you answered. Your own answers; nobody checked them. The cut-offs are the team's judgement, not tested with hotels." An area gets a word (Strong ≥ 75%, Gaps to close 50 to 74%, Weak < 50%) only with two or more known answers.

### 5.3 The four verdicts (`evaluate()`), checked in this order

| # | Verdict | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Not enough information yet** | no questions, or more than 50% of all questions are "Don't know yet" (no score shown) |
| 2 | **Not ready yet** | any must-have is No; or the score is below 50%; or fewer than half of the must-haves are Yes (`ALMOST_MIN_MUST_YES` = 50) |
| 3 | **Ready to start a pilot** | every must-have Yes, and score ≥ 75%, and fewer than 25% "Don't know yet" — **unless held back** (next row) |
| 4 | **Almost ready** | everything else, including every held-back "Ready" |

**The top verdict is held back (becomes "Almost ready") with the reason shown** when any of these holds (each a named switch in `JUDGEMENT`, all team judgement): the use is high-risk under the AI Register's provisional label or the workbook flags sensitive personal data (`LEGAL_HOLDS_TOP`: "Held back: legal items to check."); a No was carried from step 2 questions 2 to 5 (`CARRIED_NO_HOLDS_TOP`: "Held back: a No in step 2."); any step 2 answer is Not sure (`NOT_SURE_HOLDS_TOP`: "Held back: Not sure in step 2."); fewer than half of the answered tailored questions are Yes (`READY_MIN_TAIL_YES` = 50: "Held back: fewer than half the questions for this use case are Yes."); no use case was chosen (`NONE_BEST_ALMOST`: "Held back: no use case chosen.").

**Verdict lines** (shown under the word): Ready: "Every must-have is in place and most of what this use case needs is there. Start small, with the KPI and its target in front of you."; Almost ready: "Nothing you answered blocks a start, but points are still open. Close the ones below before you sign anything."; Not ready yet: "The list below shows what to do first."; Not enough information yet: "Find out first, then come back: the list below says whom to ask.". Under "Ready": "A pilot: a trial with one team or one outlet, for a set time (for example 8 to 12 weeks, the team’s suggestion), measured against the number from must-have 3."

### 5.4 The action plan

Every must-have or tailored question that is not Yes becomes an action for the person named as its owner (General manager, Owner or head office, Finance, IT, The vendor, Data protection contact, HR, Head of the department using it, Staff who will use it). Wording by answer: No → the action text; Planned → "Finish: …" (with the date if given); Don't know yet → "Find out: …"; tailored items read as the team's question ("Make this a Yes: …"). Order: any hold first ("Settle this first: …"), then all open must-haves, then open tailored questions; within each group No comes first, then Don't know yet, then Planned, then the order asked. The first three form "Start with these three" (or "this one" / "these two"). **Vendor questions** come from the AI Register's question set for the chosen use case, extended with extra questions when must-have 4 (systems that connect), must-have 6 (a person who checks the AI), must-have 5 (data protection contact) or the payment add-on is not Yes.

## 7. How to go through the flow as an AI

You can play the tool's role. Keep a small state object and follow these rules. Ask **one screen at a time**, in plain words, and show the options exactly as written.

```text
state = { hotel:{type,name,role,country}, Q:{q1..q5}, A:{m1..m10}, By:{}, use, T:{<question id>:answer}, TBy:{} }
1. Cover: show "Why this tool / How to use it / What you get" (section 4.0) in two or three sentences; ask the person to think of ONE problem in their hotel.
2. Step 1: ask how the hotel is run (required); optionally name, role, country.
3. Step 2: ask the five questions in order with Yes / No / Not sure. Give the "Yes if / No if" boxes when asked "why". If Q1 = Yes: say
   "This is not suitable for AI." + the stop text (section 4.2). END (offer to change the answer, since Not sure is allowed).
4. Show the step 2 headline (section 5.1). Continue (a No on Q2..Q5 does not stop; remember it as "carried").
5. Step 3: ask the ten must-haves (section 4.3). Allowed answers: Yes / Planned, not done yet / No / Don't know yet. Every one needs an answer.
   For "Planned" you may ask for an optional date.
6. Step 4: area -> department -> use case (Appendix A), or "None of these fits my problem" (skip step 5).
7. Step 5: ask that use case's questions (Appendix B), same four answers.
8. Result: compute with section 5 (points, score rounded down, verdict, holds) and present as section 4.6: verdict word, "Score: n%", the must-haves line,
   a reason line only for "Not ready yet" or a held-back verdict, then "Start with these three", areas, action plan by person, vendor questions.
Never invent answers for the person; never claim the answers were checked; always say the cut-offs are team judgement and the AI Act label is provisional, not legal advice.
```

**Scoring recipe in code form:**

```text
pts = sum(2 for Yes, 1 for Planned, 0 for No, 0 for Don't know) over must-haves + tailored questions
max = 2 * (number of questions asked)         # "Don't know yet" still counts in max
score = floor(100 * pts / max)
unknown = number of "Don't know yet"; must = the ten must-haves; tail = answered tailored questions
if no questions or unknown*100 > 50*total:                level = "Not enough information yet"
elif any must-have == No:                                 level = "Not ready yet"
elif pts*100 < 50*max:                                    level = "Not ready yet"
elif must.yes*100 < 50*must.n:                            level = "Not ready yet"
elif must.allYes and pts*100 >= 75*max and unknown*100 < 25*total:
     held = legal-flag or carried-No(Q2..Q5) or any step-2 Not sure or (tail.yes*100 < 50*tail.answered) or use == "none"
     level = "Almost ready" if held else "Ready to start a pilot"
else:                                                     level = "Almost ready"
```

**Checks to run when testing the tool (it should do all of these):** Q1 Yes stops and saves nothing; all ten must-haves "Planned" gives "Not ready yet" (none Yes); one must-have No beats a 94% score; a Not sure in step 2 turns a would-be "Ready" into "Almost ready"; "None of these fits" tops out at "Almost ready"; a high-risk use (e.g. hr-2) tops out at "Almost ready"; old saved answers "Partly" or "Doesn't apply" show as unanswered.
