# Omnibus revision list — European AI Governance Briefing

**What this is.** On 10 Nov 2026 the briefing was checked against Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 (Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted 8 July 2026, OJ 24.7.2026) read directly from `Digital Omnibus July 2026.pdf`. The briefing's legal basis line is correct — it does use the consolidated text — but its *description* of what the Omnibus did is too narrow, and several amendments are not reflected in the substance.

Page references below are pages of the Omnibus PDF. Point numbers are the numbered points of its Article 1.

---

## The headline problem

The briefing's glossary entry reads: *Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 amending the AI Act. In force 27 Jul 2026; delayed high-risk dates, added prohibitions, SME/SMC relief.*

The actual scope: **43 amendment points, touching 36 AI Act articles, inserting 6 new articles (4a, 60a, 75a–75d), amending 2 annexes and adding 1 (Annex XIV).** The three things the briefing names are real, but they are perhaps a fifth of it.

A reader of the briefing would not learn that the Omnibus narrowed the Annex I route, created a bias-testing permission, or opened a path to limiting the Art. 9–15 requirements.

---

## What the briefing got right

Worth stating, because the core is sound and these were all verified against the text:

- Art. 4's softening, including the precise formulation that the duty does not require guaranteeing any specific level of literacy for any individual (p.16).
- The new Art. 5 prohibitions (ba) and (bb), their subject matter, and the 2 Dec 2026 application date (p.18, p.35).
- The SMC definition by reference to Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099, cited as Art. 3(14b) (p.16).
- Art. 11's simplified technical documentation for SMEs and SMCs (p.19).
- Art. 25(2)'s cooperation duty, including the carve-out where the initial provider stated the system must not be made high-risk (p.19–20).
- The Art. 99 fine tiers, and specifically that Art. 25(2)/(4) duties sit in the 3% tier — the Omnibus added exactly that hook as Art. 99(4)(da) (p.35).
- Art. 111(2) legacy treatment and the 2 Aug 2030 public-authority date; Art. 111(4)'s 2 Dec 2026 marking deadline (p.35).
- The 2 Dec 2027 and 2 Aug 2028 high-risk dates (p.35).

No factual error was found in the briefing. Everything below is omission.

---

## Section 1.2 — The pyramid

**Add to the high-risk row, Route 1.** New Art. 6(1a)–(1c) and the redefined "safety component" in Art. 3(14) materially narrow the Annex I route (p.18, p.16).

AI solely used for non-safety-related aspects of user assistance, performance optimisation, service efficiency, automation, convenience or quality control does not qualify as a safety component. Art. 6(1b) claws this back where failure or malfunction would endanger health and safety. Art. 6(1c) excludes products needing third-party assessment solely for non-health-and-safety risks, such as spectrum or electromagnetic interference.

**Why it matters for hospitality:** this is the first Omnibus change that helps hotels directly. Building-management AI — energy optimisation, convenience automation, quality control — is now expressly outside the safety-component definition unless it is safety-critical. Section 3's table does not currently discuss Annex I at all, and arguably should, if only to say that the carve-out removes most of it.

**Add to the classification nuance paragraph.** Art. 3(14) now specifies that a component fulfils a safety function where its intended purpose is to prevent or mitigate risks to health and safety of persons or property.

---

## Section 1.4 — Timeline

**Add a row:** 27 Jul 2026 — Arts. 102–110 apply (new Art. 113(3)(d), p.35). The briefing's timeline has the Omnibus entering force on that date but does not note that it brought those articles into application.

**Expand the 2 Dec 2026 row.** It currently names the new prohibitions and the marking deadline. Art. 113(3)(a) also defers **Art. 5(1a) and (1b)** to that date — the interpretive paragraphs for points (ba) and (bb), which the briefing does not mention.

---

## Section 1.5 — Penalties

**Add to the note.** Art. 99(1) was replaced (p.35). Two additions:

- Penalties may now include warnings and non-monetary measures, not only administrative fines.
- Member States must take the interests and economic viability of SMEs, start-ups and SMCs into account when imposing penalties. This is distinct from, and additional to, the Art. 99(6)/(6a) lower-amount caps the briefing already covers.

**Add to the enforcement paragraph.** New Arts. 75a–75d (p.28 onwards) give the AI Office its own supervisory and enforcement powers, including commitments, non-compliance fines and periodic penalty payments, with safeguards. The briefing's framing — enforcement shared between the AI Office and national market surveillance authorities — is no longer the whole picture.

---

## Section 2.2 — Obligations by role and risk step

**Add Art. 17 to the provider requirements list.** Art. 17(2) was replaced (p.19): quality management system implementation must be proportionate to the size of the provider's organisation, particularly for SMEs, start-ups and SMCs, while preserving the required rigour and level of protection. This belongs beside the Art. 9–15 one-liners, and it is relief the briefing's SME/SMC discussion should claim.

**Amend the Art. 10 one-liner.** Art. 10(1) and (6) were replaced and **Art. 10(5) was deleted** (p.19). Para 5 was where the bias-detection permission for special-category data lived; it has moved to the new Art. 4a. Art. 10 now cross-refers to Art. 4a(1).

**Amend the Art. 11 one-liner.** Beyond the simplified form, the Commission must establish it and **notified bodies must accept it** (p.19). That acceptance duty is the part that makes the relief real.

**Flag a new uncertainty.** New Art. 2(13) (p.16) allows the application of Arts. 9–15 and 17–25 to be **limited** for Art. 6(1) Annex I high-risk systems, where Annex I Section A legislation provides equivalent or higher protection of health, safety or fundamental rights, and provided overall protection is not reduced. The Commission must adopt delegated acts by **2 August 2027** specifying which systems, which obligations, the conditions and the scope.

This is a genuine open question about how much of Chapter III survives for Annex I systems, and it belongs in Open items.

---

## Section 2.3 — How a deployer becomes a provider

The five routes stand. Two additions.

**Enumerate the Art. 25(2) cooperation duty.** The amended text spells it out in three limbs (p.19–20): technical documentation sufficient to assess Art. 16 compliance; informing the new provider of known limitations and failure modes; and targeted technical access including for testing and validation. The briefing's prose summary captures the substance, but the enumeration is now in the law and is worth quoting as a checklist.

**Add the open-source carve-out.** Art. 25(4) (p.20) now provides that the written-agreement duty between a high-risk provider and third-party suppliers does **not** apply to third parties making tools, services, processes or components publicly available under a free and open-source licence — other than general-purpose AI models.

**Why it matters for hospitality:** this bears directly on scenarios P1 and P5. A chain building on open-source components has no Art. 25(4) agreement duty towards those suppliers; a chain building on a commercial LLM API does. The GPAI exclusion means the carve-out does not rescue the model relationship itself.

---

## Section 3.1 — The GDPR layer

**Add a row for the new Art. 4a** (p.17). This is the most significant omission in the briefing, because it sits exactly on the AI Act/GDPR seam the section exists to describe.

Providers of high-risk systems may exceptionally process special categories of personal data where strictly necessary for bias detection and correction under Art. 10(2)(f)–(g), subject to six cumulative conditions: the objective cannot be met with other data including synthetic or anonymised; technical limits on re-use plus state-of-the-art security and pseudonymisation; documented strict access controls; no transmission to or access by other parties; deletion once the bias is corrected or retention ends; and documented necessity in the Art. 30 records of processing.

Art. 4a(2) extends an equivalent option to deployers of high-risk systems, and to providers and deployers of other systems, where bias is likely to affect health and safety, negatively affect fundamental rights, or lead to prohibited discrimination. It expressly creates no obligation to conduct bias detection.

**Why it matters for hospitality:** scenarios D4 and P2 both turn on bias in hiring data. The briefing tells a hotel it must assess and mitigate bias under Art. 10 but gives it no lawful route to the protected-characteristic data needed to do so. Art. 4a is that route, and it comes with conditions a hotel would need to design for.

**Consider adding Art. 27.** The briefing does not mention the fundamental rights impact assessment at all. Art. 27(1) was not amended, so its scope still excludes most hotels — it binds public bodies, private providers of public services, and deployers of Annex III 5(b)/(c) systems. But a hotel deploying guest credit scoring is an Annex III 5(b) deployer and would be caught, and the Omnibus amended Art. 27(4)–(5) (p.20) to let the FRIA cross-refer to or incorporate parts of a GDPR Art. 35 DPIA, with an AI Office questionnaire template to come. One sentence noting that hotels generally escape it, with that exception, would close the gap.

## Section 4 — Use-case scenarios

- **D4, P2:** add Art. 4a as the lawful route for bias testing. Add Art. 17(2) proportionality to P2's obligation list.
- **P1, P5:** add the Art. 25(4) open-source carve-out.
- **P6:** the prototyping boundary is unchanged, but note Art. 60 was amended and a new **Art. 60a** (p.25) governs real-world testing of high-risk AI covered by Annex I Section B legislation outside regulatory sandboxes, supervised under Art. 76(1). The briefing's line that real-world testing is not excluded is right; there is now a named regime for part of it.

## Section 6 — Glossary

**Rewrite the Digital Omnibus entry.** Suggested replacement:

> **Digital Omnibus on AI** — Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, adopted 8 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026. Amends the AI Act across 43 points: postpones the high-risk application dates to 2 Dec 2027 (Annex III) and 2 Aug 2028 (Annex I); adds prohibitions on non-consensual intimate imagery and CSAM material; narrows the Annex I safety-component route; inserts Art. 4a on processing special categories of personal data for bias detection; opens a path under Art. 2(13) to limiting the Art. 9–15 and 17–25 requirements for Annex I systems; adds SME, start-up and SMC relief on documentation, quality management and fines; and gives the AI Office direct enforcement powers under Arts. 75a–75d.

**Add entries for:** Art. 4a bias-detection processing; safety component (as redefined in Art. 3(14)); FRIA (Art. 27).

**Check the "safety component" usage** anywhere it appears, against the new Art. 3(14) definition.

## Open items — additions

1. **Art. 2(13) delegated acts.** Scope of the possible limitation of Arts. 9–15 and 17–25 for Annex I high-risk systems is unknown until the Commission acts, due 2 Aug 2027.
2. **Annex I restructure.** Section A point 1 was deleted and machinery added to Section B as Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 (p.36). Machinery therefore moved between sections, which changes its conformity-assessment routing and brings it inside the new Art. 60a testing regime. Which hotel building systems now sit in Section A versus Section B is not worked through.
3. **AI Office enforcement powers.** Arts. 75a–75d are new and the research does not cover them.
4. **Art. 27 FRIA.** Absent from the research; scope-limited but live for Annex III 5(b) deployers.

# Second pass — 10 November 2026

Added after working the open items. Three were closed from the regulation texts, the draft classification guidelines were read for the first time, and interim postures were written for the three items that cannot be settled.

## Correction to the first pass

**Your consultation closing date of 23 July 2026 is correct.** The first pass flagged it against two law-firm sources citing 23 June. Those sources predate an extension. The Commission's own consultation page states: opening 19 May 2026, closing 23 July 2026, and explains the consultation was originally open for six weeks until 23 June but was extended by four weeks following requests from several associations and other stakeholders. No change needed. The briefing was more accurate than the secondary commentary.

The same page also fixes the timing for Open items: **the final guidelines will be adopted by the end of 2026.** That is firmer than "final version pending" and worth stating.

## Three open items closed

**Annex I (was oi6).** Hotel exposure under the Annex I route is now thin. Combining the new Art. 6(1a) carve-out for AI used solely for non-safety purposes, Art. 6(1c) for products assessed only for spectrum or EMI risks, and the machinery move to Section B, what remains is safety-critical AI in lifts (Directive 2014/33/EU), gas appliances (Regulation (EU) 2016/426) and pressure equipment (Directive 2014/68/EU). Section 3 could say this in two sentences and close the question.

**AI Office enforcement powers (was oi7).** They do not reach hotels. Art. 75(1) as replaced gives the AI Office exclusive competence over providers of same-provider GPAI-based systems and of VLOP/VLOSE systems, and over deployers only where the deployer is also the provider or part of the same undertaking. A hotel is neither — including a chain building on a third-party LLM API, since the model provider and the system provider are different undertakings. Hotels stay with national market surveillance authorities. Two indirect channels remain and are worth a sentence each: the new powers are the regime facing your AI vendors, and new Art. 75(2a) lets a national authority refer a matter to the AI Office, which must respond within four months.

**Art. 27 FRIA (was oi8).** Hotels owe no FRIA for employment systems, because Art. 27(1) reaches only public bodies, private providers of public services, and Annex III 5(b)/(c) deployers. The trigger is narrow: scoring an individual guest, or a sole trader in their own right. Per the draft guidelines, corporate-account credit assessment is outside 5(b) altogether, including where an owner is assessed to back a company loan, because the primary beneficiary is the company. One sentence in Section 3.1 closes this.

## The draft classification guidelines — four findings that change Section 3 and Section 4

Only the Annex III document was read, and within it the horizontal issues and the employment area. The General Principles and Annex I documents were not read. These four are the ones that matter for hospitality.

**1. Point 4's personal scope is far wider than employees.** It covers employment, workers' management *and access to self-employment*. Work-related contractual relationships are not limited to formal employment contracts: freelancers, independent professionals, service providers, solo self-employed persons comparable to workers, and platform workers regardless of contractual status are all in scope wherever AI mediates or conditions their access to work opportunities. "Workplace" is read broadly, including virtual spaces.

Hospitality runs on agency staff, extras, seasonal contractors, freelance events and F&B crew and platform-sourced labour. The briefing frames point 4 around employees throughout. This widens D4, D5 and P2 and deserves its own line in Section 3.

**2. A human signing off does not change the classification.** "Decisions" in 4(b) is read functionally and expressly includes cases where a human operator formally decides but significantly relies on the AI output. Omissions count too, including a failure to promote. Separately, for Art. 6(2) generally, human involvement cannot change the intended purpose and so has no effect on classification; human oversight is an Art. 14 compliance requirement, not a classification escape, and a provider cannot make a system low-risk by bolting on a human-review requirement.

This closes off the most common intuition a hotel manager will have, and it belongs next to D4 and D5.

**3. Working time and leave are terms of the work-related relationship.** Terms include pay entitlements, working time arrangements, leave rights and resting periods, and the guidelines give two express examples of decisions on them: denial of a leave request, and a significant change to regular working hours.

This is probably a larger hospitality exposure than the performance-based allocation D5 currently describes. Rota and workforce-management software that changes regular working hours or decides leave requests is in 4(b) whether or not it scores anyone. Conversely there is a genuine floor: allocating office space, and break or lunch timing within an already-assigned shift, sits outside 4(b) so long as total break and rest time does not change. That is the usable line for the briefing's open question about allocating shifts by availability alone.

**4. AI-driven non-renewal of a fixed-term contract is termination.** Termination covers dismissal, redundancy and non-renewal of fixed-term contracts where the decision not to renew is AI-driven.

Seasonal hospitality runs on fixed-term contracts. The briefing identifies seasonal hiring as the principal exposure through recruitment under 4(a); this adds the end of the season — who does not get brought back — as a 4(b) case. Consider a scenario D8 for it; it is at least as likely as D4 and currently unrepresented.

Four further findings are recorded in the data file: split architectures and agentic assemblies are classified as a whole (relevant to P1); the Art. 6(3) filter must be read narrowly; training assessment falls in 4(b) where it gates progression or continued employability; and point 4(a) covers prospecting and pre-application screening, with a nuanced treatment of job-description generation that sits close to the line for hotels writing seasonal ads with a general chatbot.

## Interim postures

Written out in the data file for the three unsettleable items. The one worth reproducing in the briefing is the rule for handling the draft guidelines, because it is a defensible methodological position rather than a hedge:

> Adopt the draft's exposure-widening interpretations now; do not bank its exposure-narrowing interpretations as settled. A narrowing reading that disappears in the final version leaves a deployer non-compliant; a widening reading that is relaxed leaves it merely over-prepared. The costs are asymmetric, so the treatment should not be symmetric either.

For the DPF, five conditional rules are recorded. The practical point for Section 3.1 is that invalidation is a vendor-switching problem rather than a paperwork problem: the actionable step — contracting for EU hosting, or at minimum knowing where each vendor processes — is taken before any ruling, not after.

For Art. 2(13), the posture is to assume no limitation, and the mitigating observation is that after the Art. 6(1a) carve-out it barely touches hotels anyway — and not machinery at all, since machinery left Section A.
