## 1. What do credible indexes and assessments publish?

### Takeaway
Credible indexes publish the instrument itself (exact questions), who built and paid for it, the sample or benchmark base, the scoring rules with weights and cut-offs, a robustness check, a limitations statement and a version date. The maturity-model literature adds that the design process must be documented and the model tested with real users before release; the most common failing found in reviews is that authors do not disclose how design decisions were made.

### Cited Findings

**OECD/JRC composite-indicator guidance**
- The Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators was prepared jointly by the OECD (Statistics Directorate; Directorate for Science, Technology and Industry) and the JRC's Applied Statistics and Econometrics Unit in Ispra; the 2008 edition opens with "Steps for constructing a composite indicator" and a theoretical-framework section, and includes a quality-framework chapter. [secondary] — [OECD publication page](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/handbook-on-constructing-composite-indicators-methodology-and-user-guide_9789264043466-en.html); [JRC PDF](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/jrc47008_handbook_final.pdf)
- The JRC's 10-step pocket guide starts with defining objectives and end-users, mapping the literature and involving stakeholders; later steps cover data treatment, statistical and conceptual coherence, weighting, and visualisation and communication (step 10). [secondary] — [JRC 10-step pocket guide](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/10-step-pocket-guide-to-composite-indicators-and-scoreboards.pdf)
- Step 8 (uncertainty and sensitivity analysis): sensitivity analysis "quantifies the uncertainty caused by each individual assumption"; the two analyses together are presented as a way to gauge robustness, "increase its transparency" and frame discussion. The uncertain choices listed include the choice of weights (equal weights, factor analysis, expert opinion) and of aggregation (additive, multiplicative, multi-criteria). [secondary] — [JRC step 8](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/composite-indicators/10-step-guide/step-8-sensitivity-analysis_en)
- A JRC text states that after such analysis the composite indicator "is no longer a magic number corresponding to crisp data treatment, weighting set or aggregation method, but reflects uncertainty and ambiguity in a more transparent and defensible fashion". [secondary] — [JRC repository record JRC31473](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC31473)
- The JRC offers an "Audit your index" service and a COIN tool (Excel) for checking the soundness of composite indicators. [secondary] — [JRC Week on composite indicators](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/composite-indicators/2016-jrc-week-composite-indicators-scoreboards_en)

**Maturity-model design literature**

The motivation named is "incomplete documentation of methodologies applied for maturity model development". The German companion paper says a new model must be justified by comparison with existing ones and developed iteratively (design science). [secondary] — [RePEc record, DOI 10.1007/s12599-009-0044-5](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:binfse:v:1:y:2009:i:3:p:213-222); [German version, Springer](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11576-009-0167-9); [AIS record](https://aisel.aisnet.org/bise/vol1/iss3/1)
- de Bruin, Freeze, Kulkarni & Rosemann (2005), ACIS: six generic phases: scope, design, populate, test, deploy, maintain. Order matters, and test results can force a return to earlier decisions. [secondary] — [ACIS 2005 paper](https://aisel.aisnet.org/acis2005/109)
- Mettler (2011), "Maturity assessment models: a design science research approach", *Int. J. Society Systems Science* 3(1/2), 81–98: adds decision parameters for building and testing, and a user perspective. Associated observation: most authors of maturity models "did not disclose their research methods or the underlying design decisions". [secondary] — [University of St. Gallen repository](https://www.alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/a79e8841-c386-4481-a6ee-3e9c3332dee7/content)
- Pöppelbuß & Röglinger (2011), ECIS: design principles grouped by purpose of use (basic, descriptive, prescriptive). A 2012 follow-up on 10 BPM maturity models found basic and descriptive principles met, but "the design principles for a prescriptive use ... are hardly met". [secondary] — [ECIS 2011 paper](https://aisel.aisnet.org/ecis2011/28/); [Röglinger, Pöppelbuß & Becker 2012](https://www.fim-rc.de/Paperbibliothek/Veroeffentlicht/352/wi-352.pdf)

### Inferences
- A common core appears across all of these: (1) the instrument, word for word; (2) who made it and who paid; (3) the base behind any benchmark; (4) scoring rules, weights and cut-offs; (5) a robustness or validation step; (6) limits; (7) version and date. A vendor index (Cisco) and a public tool (EU DMA) both meet most of these, so a student tool that meets them is not over-reaching; one that does not will look weaker than a vendor's.
- The readiness check is "prescriptive" in Pöppelbuß & Röglinger's sense (it tells the hotel "do not buy yet"). That is the category where published models are weakest, and where the user-journey review found the tool weakest ("diagnoses but does not direct"). [repo: `review_user_journey.md` section 3]
- The tool's equal weighting of six areas with 2 to 9 questions each is exactly the kind of assumption JRC step 8 says to test. A cheap equivalent of a sensitivity analysis: re-score every saved check under two or three alternative rules (question-weighted average; thresholds at 35/70 and 45/80; must-have cap on or off) and report how many verdicts change.
- ISO 9004 gives a useful wording precedent: a self-assessment is for prioritising improvement, not for certification. The tool's "items to check, never a compliance verdict" line in the AI Register follows the same logic. [repo: `tools/register/README.md`]
- The EU DMA's T0/T1/T2 design is a precedent for the tool's "date to re-check" idea.

### Gaps
- The full ten step titles of the OECD/JRC handbook and its exact wording on weights could not be confirmed (source not reachable). Do not quote the handbook verbatim before opening it.
- Cisco's cut-offs for Pacesetters, Chasers and Followers, the full weight table and any limitations statement were not confirmed from Cisco's own page. Sources also differ on the count of markets and industries.
- EU DMA scoring formula (0–100 scale, weights per dimension) not confirmed; one national portal describes 0–10 per question and 0–100 per dimension, which may be a local implementation. Dimension names differ between sources.
- The exact numbered list of Becker et al.'s requirements (commonly cited as eight, R1–R8) was not confirmed in this session. Mettler's list of decision parameters also not retrieved.
- No source found on how often indexes publish a change log or a conflict-of-interest statement as such. AAPOR's sponsor-disclosure item is the nearest standard.
- Whether ISO/IEC 42001 offers a recognised self-assessment distinct from certification: not found.

## 2. What does research say about trust in online tools, calculators and AI-related advice?

### Takeaway
Two things carry trust: perceived expertise and perceived honesty. Being easy to verify, showing the real organisation behind the tool and admitting limits all help; confidence that later proves wrong costs credibility, and stating uncertainty costs little trust in the source. Evidence specific to hotel managers using self-assessment tools was not found.

### Cited Findings
- Stanford Guidelines for Web Credibility (Fogg, Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab, 2002): ten guidelines from three years of research with over 4,500 people. One guideline: "Make it easy to verify the accuracy of the information on your site." Third-party summaries add: show the real people and organisation behind the site. Credibility is described as two dimensions, trustworthiness (unbiased, fair, honest) and expertise. A related study found ordinary users weighted visual surface cues more than content, unlike experts. [secondary] — [Stanford guidelines](https://credibility.stanford.edu/guidelines); [Consumer Reports/Stanford expert vs. consumer study](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/05/expert-vs-online-consumers.pdf)
- Tenney, Spellman & MacCoun (2008), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, "The benefits of knowing what you know (and what you don't): How calibration affects credibility": in two experiments neither confidence nor accuracy alone explained credibility; credibility tracked calibration (being confident only where right). Base: participants judging two opposing witnesses, not managers. [secondary] — [eScholarship copy](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1ww5g76g/qt1ww5g76g.pdf?t=mxcgw5)
- Sah, Moore & MacCoun (2013), *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 121(2), 246–255: accurate advisers gain from showing confidence; "confident but inaccurate advisors receive low credibility ratings" (Study 1). When feedback on accuracy is unavailable or costly, confident advisers persuade regardless of accuracy (Study 2). [secondary] — [Stanford Law record](https://www.law.stanford.edu/publications/cheap-talk-and-credibility-the-consequences-of-confidence-and-accuracy-on-advisor-credibility-and-persuasiveness); [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jobhdp:v:121:y:2013:i:2:p:246-255)
- van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman & Spiegelhalter (2020), PNAS: five experiments, 5,780 participants, including a BBC News field study. Stating uncertainty as a numeric range caused a small drop in trust in the number and little or no drop in trust in the source; verbal vagueness ("about", "estimated") cost more. Base: general public reading news figures. Summaries differ on whether the source-trust drop for numeric ranges was significant. [secondary] — [ScienceDaily, 23 Mar 2020](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200323152106.htm); [paper summary](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/paper-summary-the-effects-of-communicating)
- Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey (2015): people who saw an algorithm make an error then preferred a human forecaster, even when the algorithm was better overall ("algorithm aversion"). A 2016 follow-up: people will use imperfect algorithms if they can modify them. Base: lab participants forecasting MBA applicant success. [secondary] — [review in AI & Society, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-023-01649-6)
- Logg, Minson & Moore (2019), OBHDP 151, 90–103: people often weighted algorithmic advice above human advice ("algorithm appreciation"); the effect weakened when the alternative was their own judgement. Later reviews treat the two results as boundary conditions: aversion appears mainly after visible mistakes and falls when people are told how the tool performs. [secondary] — [review in AI & Society, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-023-01649-6); [bidt overview](https://en.bidt.digital/phenomena/algorithm-aversion/)
- Edelman AI flash poll (2025): 78% of people in the US trust "someone like me" to tell the truth about generative AI. Base: US general population, not managers; sample size not retrieved. The 2025 Trust Barometer material also reports that trust in scientists remains high globally. [secondary] — [Edelman AI flash poll top findings](https://www.edelman.com/sites/g/files/aatuss191/files/2025-11/AI%20Flash%20Poll_Top%2010%20Findings_White.pdf); [Edelman UK trust report on science](https://info.edelman.co.uk/the-trust-report-science-needs-to-earn-influence)
- Cisco's choice of a double-blind design run by an independent partner shows that even a vendor index treats independence from the sponsor as something to demonstrate. [secondary] — [Cisco methodology page](https://www.cisco.com/c/m/en_us/solutions/ai/readiness-index/methodology.html)

### Inferences
- **Over-claiming backfires when the reader can check.** A group CIO can check: the user-journey review reached "100/100, Ready to scale" by typing "x" as proof, and got "39, Build foundations first" for a pricing tool run for three years because one text box was empty. [repo: `review_user_journey.md` sections 3, 4 and bug 5] By the Dietvorst result, one visible error of that kind is enough for an expert to discard the tool; by Sah et al., a confident verdict that is visibly wrong lowers credibility more than a modest one would.
- **Stating limits is cheap.** The van der Bles result suggests "team judgement, not yet tested with hotels" costs little trust in the team. Precise labels (what exactly is uncertain and why) are better than vague hedging.
- **A score out of 100 implies more precision than 2 to 9 three-level answers per area can give.** Showing the verdict band and the open must-haves as the headline, and the number as secondary, is the calibrated presentation.
- **Letting users see and adjust the working raises acceptance** (Dietvorst 2016). The team view already shows every step of the arithmetic; a client-side "how your result was worked out" panel would use that same strength.
- **Institutional signals available to this team:** the school (EHL), the association (HFTP), named reviewers, and peer hotels that piloted it. "People like me" evidence points to peer use (other GMs) as a stronger signal than polish. No vendor funding is itself a claim worth stating, but only if true and stated precisely (for example: "No vendor paid for, reviewed or has access to this tool or its data").
- Surface quality still matters for non-expert users (Stanford/Consumer Reports finding), so the bugs that look like errors (lower-cased acronyms, "see D7", capped-score line) are trust issues, not cosmetics.

### Gaps
- No study found on how hotel general managers, owners or association boards judge self-assessment tools or benchmarks specifically. All behavioural evidence above is from general-population or lab samples; transfer to senior hospitality managers is an inference.
- No quantified evidence found on the effect of a university or association logo on managers' trust in a tool.
- No evidence found on managers' trust in vendor-run versus independent readiness assessments.
- Exact effect sizes for the van der Bles and Sah studies were not retrieved; do not quote numbers beyond those above.
- The exact wording of the Stanford guidelines on "real organization" and "expertise" was not confirmed.

## 3. Privacy and data protection as a trust factor

### Takeaway
The tool stores a contact person's name and role, staff names inside proof notes and budget lines, and shares them with the team with no notice before the user starts. Under GDPR Art. 13 (and the Swiss FADP's Art. 19, since EHL is in Switzerland) the user must be told, at the moment of collection, who is responsible, why, who sees it, how long it is kept and how to have it deleted. A short first-layer notice plus data minimisation fixes most of this cheaply. This is practical guidance, not legal advice; the team should confirm with EHL's data protection contact.

### Cited Findings
- Current state of the tool: the result page says "Saved and shared with the EHL project team" only after the fact; the client's name, hotel and typed evidence go to a shared database "with no notice before they start"; the proof box asks for "a staff member's name and hours, budget amounts, contract dates"; "Start a new check" keeps the previous hotel name on a shared laptop. The reviewer lists data protection of the tool itself among the five items that "must be fixed before members see it". [repo] — `/home/user/final/research_notes/Hotel AI readiness check design/review_user_journey.md` (sections 1, 2, 4 item 8, bug 11)
- The AI Register prototype stores entries "in the visitor's browser only". [repo] — `/home/user/final/tools/register/README.md`
- GDPR Art. 13: information must be given when the data are obtained from the person. Paragraph 1 includes the purposes and the legal basis, and the recipients or categories of recipients. Paragraph 2 includes the storage period or the criteria used to set it, and the right to withdraw consent at any time where processing rests on consent. The duty does not apply insofar as the person already has the information. Data obtained from someone else falls under Art. 14. [secondary] — [GDPR Art. 13 text](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-13-gdpr/)
- ICO (UK) checklist for the right to be informed additionally lists: the rights available to individuals, the right to withdraw consent, the right to complain to a supervisory authority, and the source of the data if not obtained from the person; data from another source must be notified within a reasonable period, at most one month. The ICO notes its guidance is under review after the Data (Use and Access) Act of 19 June 2025. [secondary] — [ICO: what privacy information should we provide](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/what-privacy-information-should-we-provide); [ICO checklists](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/the-right-to-be-informed/checklists)
- Article 29 Working Party transparency guidelines (WP260 rev.01, adopted 29 Nov 2017, revised 11 Apr 2018, hosted by the EDPB): recommend layered notices online; the first layer should give the purposes, the controller's identity and the existence of rights, plus whatever has most impact on the person; in an app the information should never be more than "two taps away"; telling people to check back for updates is not enough. [secondary] — [WP260 rev.01](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-09/wp260rev01_en.pdf); [Hunton summary](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/2017/12/20/article-29-working-party-published-guidelines-transparency-gdpr/)
- Switzerland: under the revised FADP (in force 1 Sep 2023) the privacy statement is how a controller meets its Art. 19 duty to inform. The FDPIC's list: who is responsible, what personal data are collected, why, and whether and to whom data are passed on. The list in the law is not exhaustive; a multilingual site needs the statement in each language; using a processor does not remove the duty. [secondary] — [FDPIC: privacy statements on the internet](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/en/privacy-statements-on-the-internet)
- AAPOR's disclosure list treats data-quality screening (straight-lining, speeders) as something to report, which is relevant to how the team view flags weak proof. [secondary] — [AAPOR TI items](https://aapor.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/TI_Items-for-Immediate-Disclosure.pdf)

### Inferences (practical, not legal advice)
- **What is personal data here.** A hotel's name and its budget are company data, not personal data in themselves. The contact person's name and role, any staff member named in a proof note ("Maria Rossi, revenue manager, 4 hours a week") and free text that identifies a person are personal data. The named staff member did not enter the data, so for that person the stricter "obtained from someone else" route (Art. 14) would apply. The cheapest fix is to not collect names: ask for a **role**, not a name, in proof lines and say so in the placeholder.
- **Who is the controller must be decided, not assumed.** Candidates are EHL, the student team members, or HFTP once it hosts the tool. This needs one email to EHL's data protection contact and one line of agreement with HFTP. Until it is settled the notice cannot be written truthfully.
- **Both regimes are likely in play:** Swiss FADP because the team and school are in Lausanne, GDPR because the tool is offered to hotels in EU countries. Writing the notice to the Art. 13 list covers the FADP's shorter list.
- **A workable first-layer notice (about 60 words) before the first question:** who runs it (named team, school, client), what is saved (hotel name, your role, your answers and proof notes), why (to give you a result and to produce anonymised findings for the report), who can see it (the named team members and coach; no vendor; HFTP sees only aggregated results), how long (deleted by a fixed date after the project ends), how to delete or correct (one email address; a delete button if feasible), and a link to the full notice. Then a tick box or an explicit "Start" that follows the notice.
- **Good practice for a student prototype:** make the hotel name optional or allow a pseudonym; role instead of name; warn "do not enter names of staff or guests" at the proof box; offer a "do not save, just show my result" mode (local only, as the AI Register does); fixed deletion date; benchmark only in aggregate and only above a minimum group size so that no hotel can be recognised; never quote a proof note in the report without written permission; clear the previous hotel's name on "Start a new check"; keep the team view behind real access control, not just a private link.
- **Why this is a trust item and not only a compliance item:** the tool itself asks the hotel whether it has "a lawful basis and a vendor agreement". A CIO will notice a tool that asks this while collecting names without a notice. Fixing it turns a weakness into a demonstration that the team practises what the check asks.
- **Budget figures** are commercially sensitive even if not personal. State plainly that they are never shown per hotel, and consider asking for a band rather than an amount.

### Gaps
- Full Art. 13 list not confirmed word for word in this session (items such as the data protection officer's contact details, transfers outside the EU/EEA, whether providing the data is required, and automated decision-making are commonly listed but were not verified here). Check the regulation text before writing the full notice.
- Exact wording of FADP Art. 19 paras 2–4 not retrieved.
- Where the shared database is hosted and whether data leave Switzerland/the EEA is not stated in the files read; this matters for the notice and was not investigated.
- No guidance found from EHL on student projects handling personal data; the team should ask the school.
- Whether consent or another legal basis is the right one for this tool was not researched; the notice needs one stated basis.
- No empirical source found that quantifies the effect of a privacy notice on managers' willingness to complete a B2B assessment.

## 4. How can "team judgement" thresholds and must-haves be validated cheaply?

### Takeaway
Three cheap steps cover most of what the design literature asks for: a structured expert review of each question and must-have, a pilot with a handful of hotels that includes thinking aloud, and a consistency check (two people from the same hotel; verdict against interview evidence). The numbers involved are small (three or more experts; 5 to 15 pilot users per round), but small pilots miss problems, so the result should be described as "piloted", not "validated".

### Cited Findings
- de Bruin et al. (2005): "test" is its own phase between populate and deploy, and results can send the designer back to earlier decisions. Becker et al. (2009) require iterative development and comparison with existing models. [secondary] — [ACIS 2005 paper](https://aisel.aisnet.org/acis2005/109); [AIS record of Becker et al.](https://aisel.aisnet.org/bise/vol1/iss3/1)
- Expert review (content validity index): Lynn (1986) is reported as advising a minimum of three experts. Cut-offs differ between secondary sources: with five or fewer experts all should rate the item relevant (I-CVI 1.00) in one account, 0.83 in another; 0.78 is the usual cut-off with six or more experts. Polit & Beck recommend a scale-level average (S-CVI/Ave) of at least 0.90. [secondary; sources disagree] — [CASRAI guide to content validity](https://casrai.org/guides/content-validity); [Polit & Beck, "Is the CVI an acceptable indicator"](https://typeset.io/pdf/focus-on-research-methods-is-the-cvi-an-acceptable-indicator-1c7cnx29u8.pdf)
- Delphi: Diamond et al. (2014) found percent agreement the most common definition of consensus, with a median threshold of 75%; many studies claiming consensus had not fixed the threshold in advance. There is no standard panel size; it is a pragmatic choice, panels are chosen on purpose rather than at random, and reporting who was eligible and how they were recruited matters more than the headcount. The threshold should be set before round one. [secondary] — [CASRAI guide to the Delphi method](https://casrai.org/guides/delphi-method); [summary of Diamond 2014](https://www.koji.so/visuals/delphi-studies-defining-consensus-diamond-2014)
- Pilot size: Willis (2005) reports 5 to 15 cognitive interviews per round as typical practice. Counter-evidence: Blair et al. found the number of problems detected rises with sample size, and Perneger et al. concluded that samples of 5–15 "may fail to uncover even common problems", recommending 30 as a default. [secondary] — [Univ. of Michigan ISR best-practice note](https://srobp.isr.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/7-2-Cognitive-Interviews_-Best-Practices_Uploaded-Oct-1-2007.pdf); [Perneger et al., Quality of Life Research](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11136-014-0752-2)
- Agreement between raters is conventionally reported as Cohen's kappa and read against Landis & Koch (1977) labels such as "substantial". [secondary] — [Survey Practice article](https://www.surveypractice.org/article/88101-conducting-small-scale-multimethod-questionnaire-evaluation)
- JRC step 8: test how results move when weights and aggregation rules change. [secondary] — [JRC step 8](https://knowledge4policy.ec.europa.eu/composite-indicators/10-step-guide/step-8-sensitivity-analysis_en)
- Current state: "No hotel has used it. No inter-rater check ... No link shown to established readiness or adoption frameworks." The reviewer's recommendation: fit to test with 5 to 8 hotels after fixes 1–7; show to HFTP members only after that test, "with the test described on the page". [repo] — `/home/user/final/research_notes/Hotel AI readiness check design/review_user_journey.md` (section 4 item 7, section 7)

### Inferences: a cheap validation plan
1. **Expert review, one round, written (about a week).** Send the question list, the must-haves per tool type, the 40%/75% thresholds and the 39/74 caps to 5 to 7 people the team is already interviewing (hotel CIO, GM of an independent, hospitality/AI consultant, cybersecurity expert, legal/governance contact, coach). For each item: essential / useful / not needed, plus one comment. For each must-have: "should a hotel be told not to buy if this is missing?" yes/no. Fix the agreement rule beforehand (for example, keep a must-have only if at least 75% say yes; with five or fewer reviewers treat anything short of full agreement as "review"). Publish the table of results, including items where reviewers disagreed. A second round on the disputed items only makes it Delphi-style; a single round is honestly called an "expert review".
   - Caution: this must not add questions to the team's interview guides (project rule). Run it as a separate short form after the interview or by email.
2. **Pilot with 5 to 10 hotels, mixed (independent / group, two or three countries, at least two tool types).** Watch at least three complete the check while thinking aloud; time them; record where they stop. Report: completion rate, median time, questions answered "Not sure" most often, verdict distribution, and how many users said the verdict matched their own view.
3. **Verdict against interview evidence.** For hotels the team has interviewed, have a team member who has not seen the tool result write the expected verdict from the interview notes, then compare. Report the count of matches (for example "7 of 9") rather than a statistic.
4. **Same-hotel, two respondents.** Ask two managers of the same hotel to complete the check separately for the same tool, in 3 to 5 hotels. Report whether verdicts match and which questions differ most. With so few pairs, report raw agreement; kappa on a sample this small would over-state precision.
5. **Sensitivity run (desk work, half a day).** Re-score all saved checks under alternative thresholds and weights; report how many verdicts change. If few change, say so; if many change, the thresholds need the expert panel's attention.
6. **Proof quality.** Count one-character or repeated proof lines and exclude or flag them before any benchmark is computed (parallels AAPOR's straight-lining disclosure).

**A credible minimum before an association puts its name on it (inference, no source prescribes this):**
- A published method note (item list from section 1).
- Privacy notice in place and controller agreed.
- Expert review by at least five named or role-described reviewers from at least three stakeholder groups, results published.
- Pilot with at least 5 hotels, described on the page, with known problems listed.
- No reproducible wrong-looking verdict (the "x" proof, the capped 39 under six green bars, "Ready to scale" without a pilot question).
- Wording agreed with HFTP: "developed by EHL students for HFTP; piloted with N hotels; not a certification, not legal or investment advice". HFTP "presents" or "commissioned" it; "HFTP-endorsed standard" would over-claim.
- A named owner and a review date after the student project ends, or a stated end-of-life date.

### Gaps
- No source found that sets a minimum validation standard for association-branded self-assessment tools; the "credible minimum" above is the researcher's judgement built from the cited practices.
- CVI cut-offs for small panels conflict between secondary sources and the primary papers (Lynn 1986; Polit & Beck 2006) were not opened. State the rule the team chose rather than citing a number as the standard.
- The Landis & Koch band limits (commonly given as 0.61–0.80 for "substantial") were not confirmed in this session.
- Okoli & Pawlowski's Delphi panel-size guidance was not found.
- Predictive validity (do hotels with a "ready" verdict succeed with the tool?) cannot be tested within the project's time; say so in the limits.

## 5. How should uncertainty be communicated in the tool?

### Takeaway
Carry the AI Register's practice into the readiness check: label every rule by what it rests on, put the label next to the item rather than in the footer, and show changes with a version and date. Specific labels cost little trust; the present mix of a precise score and a grey footer disclaimer is the weak combination.

### Cited Findings
- AI Register practice already in the repository: "It gives items to check, never a compliance verdict, and it is not legal advice"; labels "follow the EU AI Act text (as amended in July 2026, checked 17 Nov 2026)"; where the register departs from the team's booklet it shows both ("Differs from the booklet"); a dated, sourced watch list of rules still moving; a "How it works" page with method and limits; a "Known gaps" list, including that 17 market uses "come from search summaries ... not verified at source" and are marked provisional; the label file "needs sign-off by the D7 owner". [repo] — `/home/user/final/tools/register/README.md`
- Readiness check today: the footer says questions and thresholds "are team judgement still to be tested with hotels" and the header says "prototype", but "Thresholds are labelled as judgement only in the footer, in grey 12 px text. The bar legend states them as fact." No question on the client page or PDF carries a source; "D6 research" is an internal label, not a source. The reviewer lists "Honest about uncertainty" among the tool's five strengths. [repo] — `/home/user/final/research_notes/Hotel AI readiness check design/review_user_journey.md` (section 4; section 7)
- One legal reference needs checking before display: the staff-representative note cites "AI Act Art. 26(7) ... from 2 Dec 2027" in the team view only, and the reviewer asks the D7 team to confirm date and wording. [repo] — same file, section 4 item 6
- Numeric, specific statements of uncertainty reduce trust in the source little or not at all; vague verbal hedges cost more (van der Bles et al. 2020; five experiments, 5,780 participants, general public). [secondary] — [ScienceDaily summary](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200323152106.htm)
- Credibility follows calibration: confidence where warranted, doubt where not (Tenney, Spellman & MacCoun 2008). [secondary] — [eScholarship copy](https://escholarship.org/content/qt1ww5g76g/qt1ww5g76g.pdf?t=mxcgw5)
- JRC: showing how results move under different assumptions makes an index "more transparent and defensible". [secondary] — [JRC repository record](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC31473)
- ISO 9004 precedent for wording: a self-assessment tool that is explicitly not for certification. [secondary] — [ISO news](https://www.iso.org/news/Ref2187.htm)

### Inferences: a label scheme for the readiness check
Use one small chip per question, must-have, threshold and action, with the same vocabulary as the Register:

| Label | Meaning | Example in the tool |
|---|---|---|
| **Law text** | Comes from a legal article; article and check date shown | Telling guests they are talking to an AI |
| **Official guidance** | Regulator or standards-body guidance, not binding text | Risk review before launch |
| **Research** | A named survey or study; who was asked and how many shown next to it | "Hotels most often fail on data quality" |
| **Interview evidence** | From the team's interviews; number and type of interviewees shown | Must-have "named owner" |
| **Team judgement** | The team's decision; not tested | Thresholds 40% / 75%; caps 39 / 74; points 2/1/0 |
| **Piloted** | Team judgement that has been through the expert review or hotel pilot; date and N shown | A must-have kept after review |

Further points:
- **Put the label where the claim is.** The bar legend should read "75% or more: ready (team judgement, not yet tested with hotels)" until piloted.
- **Headline the band, not the number.** Verdict and "blocked by N must-haves" first; the average as supporting detail. Avoid a bare "39/100".
- **Name the verdict for what the questions measure.** "Ready to pilot" or "prepared", not "Ready to scale", unless a pilot-result question is added.
- **State what the check cannot tell you** in three lines on the result and the PDF: self-reported answers, no check of the vendor's product, no forecast of return.
- **Say when there is no benchmark.** "No comparison with other hotels yet: fewer than N have completed this check" is more credible than silence.
- **Version and date on every output** (rules v2, 18 Nov 2026) and a short public change log; the team view already recomputes under current rules and flags changed scores, which is the raw material for this.
- **Translate internal labels.** "D6", "see D7", "New question", "gates" must become sources or plain words before any client sees them.
- **Legal labels need the D7 owner's sign-off**, as the Register already requires.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests provenance labels of this kind (law / guidance / judgement) on business users; the scheme rests on general findings about uncertainty communication plus the team's existing practice.
- The files read do not show the readiness check's own README or rules file, so the mapping of each existing question to a label has not been done here.
- The AI Act date cited in the team view (Art. 26(7), 2 Dec 2027) was not verified in this research.

### Inferences
- **Order of work.** Items 1–8 are the "before any hotel sees it" set and overlap with the user-journey review's changes 2, 3, 5, 6 and 7. Items 9–12 are the "before HFTP members see it" set. Items 13–15 are the "before HFTP's name stays on it" set.
- **For the report**, the same list becomes a one-page method box: who built it, what it rests on (with labels), how it was tested (N reviewers, N hotels, dates), what changed after testing, what it cannot tell you. Every N carries its base.
- **Wording to avoid until earned:** "validated", "benchmark", "index", "certified", "HFTP standard", "ready to scale". Wording that is safe now: "prototype", "self-assessment", "team judgement", "items to check", and after items 9–10, "expert-reviewed" and "piloted with N hotels".
- The strongest single trust move is to let the tool pass its own test: it asks hotels for a named owner, a lawful basis, a baseline and a stop rule; the method box can show the tool's own owner, privacy basis, pilot baseline and review date.

- The minimum group size for anonymised benchmarking was not researched; a figure should be agreed with EHL's data protection contact.
