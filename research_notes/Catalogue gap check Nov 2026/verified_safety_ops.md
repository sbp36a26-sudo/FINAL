## 1. Lost-and-found logging with AI

**What it does.** A housekeeper or front-desk agent photographs a found item with a phone. The software names the item, adds colour, category and other tags, files it with the place and time, hides card numbers and ID details in the photo, compares it with claims guests submit online or by phone, and sells the guest a shipping label.

**Is it AI?** Partly, and it depends on the vendor. The photo-to-description step is image recognition (computer vision); BOUNTE, Chargerback, NotLost, Boomerang and Lost and Found Software all say so on their own pages. Matching items to claims is described as AI by BOUNTE and Boomerang without explaining the method. Payment, labels and chain of custody are ordinary workflow software. iLost and Faundit, two European tools aimed at hotels, describe the same workflow and **do not mention AI at all** on the pages opened.

**Overlap.** None. The catalogue has no lost-and-found row. The nearest row, "Multilingual task capture and staff messaging", is about turning speech into tasks.

## 2. Bed-bug detection monitors with image recognition

**What it does.** A small box sits under the mattress or by the bed. A lure draws insects in; a camera photographs whatever enters; the system decides whether it is a bed bug and emails the hotel the room number and the photo. The aim is to find the first bug before a guest does.

**Is it AI?** For Spotta, yes: image recognition classifies the insect. The vendor's 2020 release says "AI-driven recognition technology" (2-A) and the Daily Mail quotes "advanced image recognition algorithms" (2-D). Spotta's current product pages, as far as their text loaded, say "hidden sensors" and "confirms a detection" and did not show the word AI. Delta Five is a heated trap with a camera that sends the photo to a person; the independent study below describes no automatic classification. Valpas now describes itself as a certification, not a trap, and does not claim AI.

**Overlap.** None. Not in Housekeeping, Maintenance or any other row.

## 3. Pool drowning detection with computer vision

**What it does.** Cameras above the pool, under water, or both feed software that follows each swimmer. When someone stops moving, stays under too long or shows signs of distress, the system alerts a lifeguard's smartwatch or a screen with the location. Systems for homes and small hotel pools alert reception or security staff instead.

**Is it AI?** Yes: computer vision with trained models. CORDIS describes "real-time video analysis algorithms" and "deep learning" (3-M); operators call it "AI-supported" (3-D, 3-E, 3-G). Wearable systems (WAVE headbands, 3-Q) are sensors with a time threshold, not AI.

**Overlap: already partly in the catalogue.** The IT & Security row "Physical security video analytics" has the words "also weapon detection on cameras and drowning alerts at pools" in its task text and lists Lynxight ("AI drowning detection at pools; the vendor reports more than 1,000 pools in 16 countries (no hotel named)"). Lynxight is also in the company list under IT & Security. What the catalogue does not yet have: any hotel example, the standard (ISO 20380), the regulator's position, or independent evidence on reliability.

**EU AI Act and GDPR (our reading, D7 to confirm).**
- **High-risk through product law (Art. 6(1), Annex I)?** Probably not. A camera-and-software pool monitor is not, as far as we can see, a product that one of the Annex I laws sends to third-party conformity assessment for health and safety reasons; ISO 20380 is a voluntary standard, not harmonisation legislation. Radio-equipment rules do not count for this purpose (Art. 6(1c) as amended). Art. 6(1b) says an AI system whose failure "would endanger health and safety" qualifies as a safety component, and a missed drowning plainly does; but a safety component only becomes high-risk under Art. 6(1) when the product sits under an Annex I law with third-party assessment. D7 should confirm that no Annex I law (for example machinery or personal protective equipment) catches these systems.
- **Annex III?** Not as described by operators. No one is identified ("The system cannot identify individuals", 3-E; "Facial recognition does not take place", 3-D), so it is not biometric identification or categorisation; it does not infer emotions; pools are not critical infrastructure under Annex III point 2.
- **Result: minimal risk for the hotel if the system only tracks movement.** That changes if a vendor adds face recognition, age estimation or "behaviour" profiles of individuals.
- **GDPR applies in full.** Video of identifiable people in swimwear, many of them children, in a place open to guests. Our reading: a data protection impact assessment before installation (systematic monitoring of an accessible area), signs at the pool, cameras on the water only, short retention, footage opened only after an incident, a processing agreement with the vendor, and no reuse of footage for marketing or model training without a separate basis. The KölnBäder and Imperial College set-ups are usable templates.
- **Liability framing.** The system is a second pair of eyes; the duty to supervise and to rescue stays with the operator (3-N). A hotel that removes or reduces pool attendants because it bought the system takes on the risk assessment burden HSE describes. A hotel pool with no lifeguard gains an alert to reception but still needs someone trained to respond within seconds. Vendors' talk of lower "liability" (for example on wavedds.com, opened) is marketing; no court decision on the point was found.

## 4. In-room smoking, vape and noise sensors that lead to guest charges (cautionary example)

**What it does.** A plug-in or ceiling sensor in the guest room measures the air (particles, gases, humidity and similar) and, in some products, sound level. Software decides that a "smoking event" or a noise breach happened and sends the hotel a time-stamped report. Hotels then add a smoking fee to the folio, in the US typically $250 to $500 or more, sometimes days after check-out.

**Is it AI?** Mostly not claimed as AI, and nothing independent shows what the software does.
- **Rest** (the brand in most of the reported disputes): "proprietary algorithm" that fires "when a change in air quality meets defined thresholds" (4-K, 4-L). No use of "AI" or "machine learning" on the pages opened.
- **FreshAir Sensor**: "molecular" polymer sensors, no AI claim (4-M).
- **Wynd Sentry**: does claim AI: "Powered by advanced AI" (4-N).
- **Minut**: says its sound analysis runs on the device using "machine learning" (4-P); its cigarette-smoke feature is described as detecting "an elevated risk of cigarette smoke" with no method (4-Q).
- **HALO** (Motorola Solutions) and **NoiseAware**: pages could not be read (see "What failed").
- One blocked headline (san.com, "How AI is charging hotel guests $500 for using a hairdryer") calls it AI; it could not be opened and is not evidence.
For the catalogue this is **sensor-and-threshold automation with an "algorithm" label**, plus one vendor that says AI.

Vendors describe a 'proprietary algorithm'; none publishes a false-alarm rate. A sensor alert should start a check by staff, not a charge."
**Example vendors (links opened):** Rest (4-J to 4-L), FreshAir Sensor (4-M), Wynd (4-N), Minut (4-O to 4-Q). HALO and NoiseAware: named in the brief, not verified.

**EU AI Act and GDPR (our reading, D7 to confirm).**
- **AI Act: minimal risk at most, and often out of scope.** A threshold on air readings may not be an "AI system" at all (the catalogue's rules-based exit); a product that does use machine learning (as Wynd claims) still falls outside Annex III: no biometrics, no emotion or identity inference, no employment, credit or essential-service decision. A hotel smoking fee is not an "essential private service". It is not a safety component: these sensors are sold as separate from the fire alarm ("A cigarette smoke device doesn't replace a code-compliant smoke alarm", 4-Q).
- **The legal weight sits in consumer law and GDPR.** Once the reading is tied to a room and a booking it is personal data about that guest, whatever the vendor says about not identifying people (4-P). Our reading of the GDPR points: guests must be told the sensor exists and what it does (Arts. 12–13; compare the guest who "had no idea the sensor was even in her room", 4-E); the data must be accurate enough for the use made of it (Art. 5(1)(d)); the guest has a right to see the reading (Art. 15; compare "I asked for reports ... I got nothing", 4-E); and a fee applied on the sensor report alone, with no room check and no chance to answer, looks like a decision based solely on automated processing with a significant effect (Art. 22), which needs a human who can change the outcome. Sound sensors in a bedroom raise proportionality even when only decibels leave the device.
- **Consumer law:** a fee the guest did not agree to clearly at booking, backed by evidence the guest cannot test, raises unfair-term and unfair-practice questions under EU consumer rules (Directives 93/13/EEC and 2005/29/EC) and national law. The burden of proving the breach would normally sit with the hotel. D7 should confirm how this plays out per country.
- **Practical rule for the report:** sensor first, person second, charge last: staff check the room at the time, the guest is told at the time, the guest sees the data, and the fee is in the booking terms.

## Cross-cutting notes for the catalogue

- **Two candidates are clearly AI (image recognition): lost-and-found photos and bed-bug classification.** Both are small, well-bounded uses with minimal legal weight, and both rest on vendor-reported results.
- **The two candidates with the most press are the weakest fits for a new row.** Drowning detection is real AI but already in the catalogue and unproven in European hotels; smoking sensors are in hotels but are mostly not AI and are documented mainly through their errors.
- **One finding worth carrying into D7:** in all four, the harm or benefit depends on what a person does after the alert (ship the item, treat the room, pull the swimmer out, check the room before charging). That matches pattern 4 in the gap note ("AI advises; people act and stay liable").
