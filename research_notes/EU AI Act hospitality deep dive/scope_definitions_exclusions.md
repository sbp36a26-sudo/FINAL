## 1. Art. 3(1) "AI system": which hotel tools are NOT AI systems, where is the line, and how can it be abused?

### Takeaway
The legal test is one sentence (Art. 3(1)) and the only hard exclusion in the regulation itself is in a recital: systems "based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations" are not AI systems. Everything finer (linear regression, spreadsheets, "simple prediction") comes from non-binding Commission guidelines that I could only see as search summaries, so "this is not AI" is a CONDITIONAL exit that needs a written technical basis per tool, and it is the exit most open to vendor abuse because the hotel cannot inspect the vendor's code.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition, unchanged by OMNI.** "'AI system' means a machine-based system that is designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy and that may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment, and that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers, from the input it receives, how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations, or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments." [text] — [BASE Art. 3(1), p.46](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **The one exclusion written by the legislator.** The definition "should not cover systems that are based on the rules defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations"; "A key characteristic of AI systems is their capability to infer"; inference "transcends basic data processing by enabling learning, reasoning or modelling". [text] — [BASE recital 12, p.4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Both machine learning and logic/knowledge-based systems are inside.** Techniques that enable inference "include machine learning approaches that learn from data how to achieve certain objectives, and logic- and knowledge-based approaches that infer from encoded knowledge or symbolic representation of the task to be solved". [text] — [BASE recital 12, p.4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Adaptiveness is optional.** The text says "may exhibit adaptiveness after deployment"; recital 12 describes it as something the system "could exhibit". A static, never-retrained model is therefore still an AI system. [text] — [BASE Art. 3(1), p.46; recital 12, p.4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Embedded or not does not matter.** AI systems "can be used on a stand-alone basis or as a component of a product, irrespective of whether the system is physically integrated into the product (embedded) or serves the functionality of the product without being integrated therein (non-embedded)". [text] — [BASE recital 12, p.4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Commission guidelines on the definition.** Published 6 February 2025; one source gives the formal reference C(2025) 5053 final dated 29 July 2025. They are non-binding. They name four groups outside the definition: systems for improving mathematical optimisation, basic data processing, systems based on classical heuristics, and simple prediction systems. [secondary] — [Inside Privacy / Covington summary](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/european-commission-guidelines-on-the-definition-of-an-ai-system/); [Matheson](https://www.matheson.com/insights/eu-commission-publishes-guidelines-on-definition-of-an-ai-system/); [Commission PDF listed on the AI Act Service Desk, not fetchable](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2026-01/guide-definition_en.pdf)
- **Examples reported from the guidelines.** Linear or logistic regression used to improve or approximate established optimisation methods (para 42); database queries, standard spreadsheet software and statistical visualisations (paras 46-47); a chess program using minimax with heuristic evaluation; a tool that always predicts the historical average. Commentators add that regression methods are excluded only where the system does not go beyond basic data processing; outside that context they are in scope. Paragraph numbers come from secondary summaries and must be checked. [secondary] — [Modulos summary of the guidelines](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/eu-ai-act/commission-guidance/definition); [Norton Rose Fulbright Data Protection Report](https://www.dataprotectionreport.com/2025/02/the-commissions-guidelines-on-ai-systems-what-can-we-infer/)
- **Criticism of the guidelines.** Matheson says it remains unclear when a system crosses from basic computation into AI; the Irish Council for Civil Liberties published a critique titled "AI system definition guidelines fail to provide clarity". [secondary] — [Matheson](https://www.matheson.com/insights/eu-commission-publishes-guidelines-on-definition-of-an-ai-system/); [ICCL](https://www.iccl.ie/news/ai-system-definition-guidelines-fail-to-provide-clarity/)
- **I found no "dashboard" example** in any summary of the guidelines; the nearest reported category is "statistical visualisations" under basic data processing. [secondary] — [Modulos summary](https://docs.modulos.ai/frameworks/eu-ai-act/commission-guidance/definition)
- **The team's data file has no entry on the Art. 3(1) boundary** (no key for it under `scope`, `boundaries` or `glossary` sections read). [team data] — [JSON `scope`, `boundaries`](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json)

### Inferences
Hotel tool sort, applying recital 12 [all my inference; no source classifies hotel tools]:

| Tool | AI system? | Why | Rating of the "not AI" claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| PMS automation rules ("if no-show by 18:00, release room"), rate fences and price rules typed in by the revenue manager, BMS/thermostat schedules, keyword or decision-tree chatbots with scripted answers, housekeeping task rules, loyalty-tier rules | No | Rules "defined solely by natural persons to automatically execute operations" (recital 12) | SAFE EXIT while every rule is human-written and nothing is learned from data |
| BI dashboards, STR/benchmark reports, pivot tables, pace reports, averages, year-on-year comparisons | No | Basic data processing; no inference of how to generate an output | SAFE EXIT for the dashboard itself; lost the moment a forecasting or anomaly-detection model is switched on inside it |
| Simple forecast = last year's same-day figure or a moving average | Probably no | "Simple prediction" per the guidelines | CONDITIONAL: rests on non-binding guidance seen only in summary |
| Revenue management system with machine-learned demand forecast and price recommendation | Yes | Learns from data, infers recommendations | Not an exit. It is an AI system, but not high-risk (pricing is not in Annex III; JSON `notHighRisk`) |
| LLM chatbot, voice agent, review-response generator, AI image tools, translation | Yes | Generates content | Not an exit |
| "Scripted" chatbot that uses a machine-learned intent classifier to pick the script | Yes | The classifier infers; the scripted answers do not change that | FALSE LOOPHOLE if sold as "rules-based" |
| CV screening by fixed keyword match written by HR | No | Human-defined rule | SAFE EXIT under the AI Act only. GDPR Art. 22 (automated decisions) still applies |
| CV ranking, staff scoring, rota optimiser that learns from individual behaviour | Yes | Inference from data | Not an exit; Annex III point 4 territory |
| Rota built by a mathematical optimiser/solver on fixed constraints | Unclear | Constraint solvers are "logic- and knowledge-based approaches" (recital 12) yet the guidelines carve out "mathematical optimisation" | CONDITIONAL and genuinely grey |
| Face matching at check-in, CCTV analytics, licence-plate recognition | Yes | Machine-learned recognition | Not an exit |

- The definition is technology-objective. Marketing does not decide it in either direction: a rule engine sold as "AI-powered" is still outside, and a learned model sold as "smart rules" is still inside. Marketing matters for *intended purpose* (Art. 3(12)), not for Art. 3(1). [my inference from the wording of Art. 3(1) and 3(12)]
- Being "not an AI system" removes every AI Act duty at once, including Art. 4 literacy and Art. 50 chatbot disclosure, because each attaches to "AI systems". It removes nothing under GDPR, labour law or consumer law. [my inference]
- **How the line can be abused or wrongly relied on** [my inference]:
  1. *Vendor self-declaration.* The hotel receives a statement "our product is rules-based, the AI Act does not apply". The Act gives the deployer no right to inspect; a regulator will look at the system, not the statement. Ask for the model inventory in writing and make it a contract warranty.
  2. *Hybrid products.* One learned component (a forecast, a classifier, an embedded LLM "copilot") makes the product an AI system for that function. SaaS vendors add such features silently in updates.
  3. *Leaning on "linear regression is excluded".* The guidelines' carve-out is reported as limited to optimisation and basic data processing; a regression that scores job applicants is not "simple" in effect and the carve-out is non-binding.
  4. *Split-system arguments.* The draft high-risk guidelines treat an assembly of components as one system for classification, expressly against circumvention by design. [team data] — [JSON `draftClassificationGuidelines.horizontalFindings` g4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json)
- Practical consequence for the booklet: the definition question only decides an outcome where the tool would otherwise be prohibited, high-risk or in Art. 50. For most back-office tools the answer changes only the Art. 4 literacy duty. [my inference]

### Gaps
- The Commission guidelines on the definition could not be read (host blocked). Exact wording, paragraph numbers and whether the July 2025 formal version differs from February 2025 are unverified. Hosts to allow: ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu, digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu.

- Whether constraint-based schedulers and solvers count as AI systems is not settled by any source found.

## 2. Art. 2 scope in full: territorial reach and every exclusion. Which matter to hotels?

### Takeaway
Art. 2 has no size threshold, no sector exclusion and no revenue test: every hotel established in the EU that uses an AI system professionally is a deployer in scope, and non-EU providers and deployers are caught when the output is used in the Union. Of the twelve-plus paragraphs, only four matter to hotels in practice: 2(1) (reach), 2(8) (pre-launch testing), 2(10) (private use) and 2(11) (national worker rules, which widens rather than narrows); the rest are safe to state in one line as "not a hotel exit".

### Cited Findings
**Who is in (Art. 2(1), not amended)**
- (a) "providers placing on the market or putting into service AI systems ... in the Union, irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the Union or in a third country"; (b) "deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are located within the Union"; (c) "providers and deployers of AI systems that have their place of establishment or are located in a third country, where the output produced by the AI system is used in the Union"; (d) importers and distributors; (e) product manufacturers placing an AI system on the market with their product under their own name; (f) authorised representatives; (g) "affected persons that are located in the Union". [text] — [BASE Art. 2(1), p.45](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Recital 22 gives the anti-circumvention example**: an EU operator contracts services to a third-country operator whose AI system processes data transferred from the Union and returns the output. "To prevent the circumvention of this Regulation ... this Regulation should also apply to providers and deployers of AI systems that are established in a third country, to the extent the output produced by those systems is intended to be used in the Union." [text] — [BASE recital 22, p.6](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Text/recital mismatch.** Art. 2(1)(c) says "is used in the Union"; recital 22 says "is intended to be used in the Union". [text] — [BASE Art. 2(1)(c), p.45; recital 22, p.6](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf). White & Case reads the article literally (no intent element; a non-EU operator may be caught wherever output is used in the EU); another commentator reads the recital's "intended" as a limit. Unresolved. [secondary] — [White & Case, "The EU AI Act's extraterritorial scope, part 2"](https://whitecase.com/sites/default/files/2024-05/the-eu-ai-acts-extra-territorial-scope-part-2.pdf); [ai-laws.org, "The Long-Arm Statute of the AI Act"](https://blog.ai-laws.org/?p=3237)
- **Third-country providers of high-risk systems must appoint an EU authorised representative** "prior to making their high-risk AI systems available on the Union market". [text] — [BASE Art. 22(1), p.65](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- The team's position: "A non-EU hotel group whose AI output is used in the EU can therefore be in scope" (confidence: settled). [team data] — [JSON `scope`](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json)

Territorial reach applied to hotel structures [all my inference from Art. 2(1) and recital 22]:

| Situation | In scope? | Basis | Confidence |

| US/UK/Swiss group HQ running one central HR-screening or staff-scoring tool whose results are used to hire or manage staff at its EU hotels | Yes, HQ is a third-country deployer (and provider if it built the tool) | 2(1)(c); this is almost exactly recital 22's example | High |

- Art. 2(1)(g) lists affected persons located in the Union as within scope, which is about their rights (for example Art. 85 complaints, Art. 86 explanation); it does not by itself make a foreign hotel an obliged operator. [my inference]
- The Act requires an EU representative for third-country *providers* of high-risk systems (Art. 22) but I found no parallel duty for third-country *deployers*. Enforcement against a non-EU deployer with no EU entity is therefore practically weak, but a hotel group with EU properties always has an EU entity that is a deployer under 2(1)(b). "Run it from head office abroad" is a FALSE LOOPHOLE. [my inference; only Art. 22 read]
- **2(8) in hotel terms.** Building and testing a guest assistant on synthetic or historical data in a test environment is outside the Act. The first pilot with real guests or real staff at one property is "putting into service ... for own use" (Art. 3(11)) or real-world testing, and the exclusion ends. Calling a live rollout a "pilot" or "beta" does not extend it. The team's scenario P6 takes the same position. [my inference; team data consistent]
- **2(10) in hotel terms.** A receptionist using a private ChatGPT account to draft a guest reply or rank CVs is acting professionally, so 2(10) does not apply. Who the deployer is (the hotel or the employee) is a separate grey question, see G9 in section 6. [my inference]
- **2(12) in hotel terms.** Three reasons it rarely helps a hotel: (i) the exclusion falls away for anything high-risk, prohibited or under Art. 50, and a guest chatbot is under Art. 50; (ii) it attaches to the system "released under" the licence, while the hotel's own assistant built on top is a new, closed system of which the hotel is provider; (iii) paid support or hosted versions are "monetised" (recital 103). What survives: an open-source, non-guest-facing, non-high-risk internal tool run as released, where even Art. 4 literacy arguably does not apply. CONDITIONAL and narrow. [my inference]
- **No de minimis.** There is no employee, revenue, room-count or usage threshold anywhere in Art. 2. A 12-room family hotel using a chatbot is a deployer. Size only affects fines and some provider paperwork (section 5). [my inference from the absence of any threshold in Art. 2, text read in full]

## 3. Definitions that decide everything, and where they are ambiguous for hotel structures

### Takeaway
The Act allocates duties by function (who develops and brands = provider; who uses "under its authority" = deployer), not by ownership, contract labels or the hotel industry's owner/operator/brand/franchisee split, and it defines neither "under its authority" nor "undertaking". For a managed or franchised hotel, several companies can each hold a role for the same tool, and the answer has to be argued from the facts of control; no source I found resolves the franchise or management-contract case.

### Cited Findings
**Role definitions (none amended by OMNI)**
- **Provider**: one "that develops an AI system ... or that has an AI system ... developed and places it on the market or puts the AI system into service under its own name or trademark, whether for payment or free of charge". [text] — [BASE Art. 3(3), p.46](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Deployer**: one "using an AI system under its authority except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity". Recital 13 repeats the definition without explaining "authority". [text] — [BASE Art. 3(4), p.46; recital 13, p.4](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Importer**: EU-established person "that places on the market an AI system that bears the name or trademark of a natural or legal person established in a third country". **Distributor**: "a natural or legal person in the supply chain, other than the provider or the importer, that makes an AI system available on the Union market". **Operator** covers all of these. Importers and distributors have their own obligations (Arts. 23, 24). [text] — [BASE Art. 3(6)-(8), p.46; Arts. 23-24 headings, pp.65-67](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Placing on the market**: "the first making available of an AI system ... on the Union market". **Making available**: "the supply of an AI system ... for distribution or use on the Union market in the course of a commercial activity, whether in return for payment or free of charge". **Putting into service**: "the supply of an AI system for first use directly to the deployer or for own use in the Union for its intended purpose". [text] — [BASE Art. 3(9)-(11), p.47](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Intended purpose**: "the use for which an AI system is intended by the provider, including the specific context and conditions of use, as specified in the information supplied by the provider in the instructions for use, promotional or sales materials and statements, as well as in the technical documentation". [text] — [BASE Art. 3(12), p.47](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/EU%20AI%20Act%20June%202024.pdf)
- **Draft guidelines on intended purpose**: if instructions, terms, marketing or documentation present a general system as broadly applicable and do not consistently exclude high-risk uses, its intended purpose is deemed to include them; "a terms-of-service exclusion alone is insufficient" (General principles draft, para 12). Classification is the provider's responsibility (para 13). Separate Art. 25 value-chain guidelines are in preparation (para 14, fn 6). Draft, non-binding. Who holds which role in typical hotel structures [all my inference; no source found addresses franchise, management-contract or lease structures]:

| Structure and tool | Likely deployer | Likely provider | Why it is ambiguous |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner-operated independent hotel buys a SaaS chatbot | The hotel company | The vendor | Clear |
| Managed hotel: owner employs the staff, management company runs the hotel and chooses the HR-screening tool | Arguable for both. The management company directs the use; the owner is the legal employer whose staff use it and whose hiring decisions result | Vendor | "Under its authority" is undefined. Is authority the power to direct the tool (operator) or the legal responsibility for the workforce (owner)? Art. 26(7) addresses "deployers who are employers", which assumes they coincide |
| Franchise: brand mandates a tool in brand standards and contracts with the vendor; franchisee's staff use it daily | Franchisee for its own use; the brand too if it uses the outputs centrally (for example group-wide guest scoring) | Vendor, unless the brand's name is on it | The brand that mandates but does not use is not a deployer on the wording, yet controls the choice. If the brand passes a third-party tool on to franchisees it may be a **distributor** (Art. 3(7)), or an **importer** (Art. 3(6)) if the vendor is non-EU and the brand's EU entity is first to place it on the Union market |
| Brand builds its own assistant or HR tool and rolls it out to owned, managed and franchised hotels | Each hotel entity that uses it | The brand (Art. 3(3): develops and puts into service under own name) | For franchised hotels the supply is plausibly "making available ... in the course of a commercial activity" = placing on the market; for wholly-owned hotels it is at least "own use". Either way provider status attaches |

For a non-high-risk tool: arguably also the brand under Art. 3(3) ("has an AI system developed and ... puts [it] into service under its own name") | Art. 25(1)(a) covers only high-risk systems. | Outsourced security firm runs video analytics in the lobby | Contractor, hotel, or both | Vendor | Art. 4 speaks of "other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf", which suggests on-behalf operators stay within the principal's sphere; nothing says so for Art. 26 |

- **"Without prejudice to contractual arrangements" (Art. 25(1)(a))** lets brand and vendor agree that the vendor keeps the provider obligations when the brand puts its name on a high-risk tool. It is the only place where a contract can move a role. CONDITIONAL: it needs an express written allocation, and it does not exist for routes (b) and (c). [my inference from the text]
- **Contract labels otherwise do not move roles.** A management agreement saying "the owner is the deployer" does not bind a market surveillance authority; the definitions are factual. Contracts can only allocate cost and indemnity between the parties. [my inference]
- **Route (c) wording gap.** Art. 25(1)(c) is triggered when someone "modif[ies] the intended purpose" of a non-high-risk system so that it becomes high-risk. A hotel that simply *uses* a general chatbot to rank applicants has not altered the product or its documentation. Whether mere use equals "modifying the intended purpose" is not stated in the text. The team treats it as settled that it does; the draft guidelines (p1) push part of the answer back to the vendor: if the vendor did not consistently exclude high-risk uses, the high-risk use is already within the vendor's intended purpose, the vendor is the provider of a high-risk system and the hotel is "only" a high-risk deployer. If the vendor did exclude it, the hotel that ignores the exclusion is the one changing the purpose. Either branch is bad for the hotel; neither is an exit. [my inference; team data for p1]
- **Vendor disclaimers.** "Not to be used for employment decisions" in a vendor's terms (a) switches off the vendor's Art. 25(2) cooperation duty if "clearly specified", and (b) on the draft guidelines is insufficient on its own to narrow the intended purpose. So the disclaimer protects the vendor against the hotel more than against the regulator, and leaves a hotel that crosses the line without documentation support. [my inference from OMNI Art. 25(2) text and team data p1]
- **Safety component after OMNI is purpose-led and provider-declared** (recital 7), with a results-based override in Art. 6(1b). A BMS vendor can describe an AI module as "energy optimisation" and stay outside; if the same module controls smoke extraction, legionella temperature or gas shut-off, failure "would endanger health and safety" and Art. 6(1b) pulls it back in whatever the label says. Rating: 6(1a) is a SAFE EXIT for comfort, energy and convenience functions; a FALSE LOOPHOLE where the function is in fact safety-relevant. [my inference]
- **Biometric lines in hotel terms** [my inference unless marked]:
  - Face match of a guest against their own passport photo at self check-in, or mobile key unlocked by face on the guest's phone: verification, outside Annex III (SAFE EXIT under the AI Act; still GDPR Art. 9 data).
  - Camera at the entrance comparing every face against a VIP or banned-guest list: one-to-many, without active involvement: remote biometric identification, high-risk from 2 Dec 2027. Calling it "verification" is a FALSE LOOPHOLE.
  - Staff clock-in by fingerprint or face: 1:1 or opt-in presentation, outside Annex III point 1 on the draft guidelines [team data b1]; national labour and data-protection law is usually the real constraint.
  - Sentiment analysis of written reviews, emails and chat: not biometric data, so not an "emotion recognition system" (SAFE EXIT under Art. 3(39), supported by team data b4).
  - Voice analytics that infer a caller's anger or a staff member's mood: biometric-based. For guests: high-risk (Annex III 1(c)) plus Art. 50(3) notice. For staff: prohibited by Art. 5(1)(f) since 2 Feb 2025.
  - Fatigue detection for night drivers or kitchen staff: a physical state, not an emotion (recital 18); and Art. 5(1)(f) has a "medical or safety reasons" exception. CONDITIONAL: the purpose must genuinely be safety, not performance monitoring.
  - "Smile detection" for a service-quality score: detection of a smile alone is outside (recital 18) "unless ... used for identifying or inferring emotions"; a "guest happiness index" built on it is inferring emotion. FALSE LOOPHOLE.
  - Age estimation at a self-service bar or minibar: categorisation by age; on the draft guidelines not point 1(b) because age is not a GDPR Art. 9 attribute [team data b5]. Art. 50(3) notice still attaches to "a biometric categorisation system" unless the Art. 3(40) "ancillary ... strictly necessary" exception applies, which is doubtful because age checking can be done without it.
- **"Undertaking".** In EU competition law the term means the economic unit, which can include a parent and its subsidiaries; if the same meaning is used here, fine ceilings for a hotel belonging to a group are calculated on group worldwide turnover, not the single property company. The Act does not say this; it is the standard reading of the word in EU fining rules. [my inference from general EU law knowledge; not verified in a source this session]

## 4. Transitional rules: Art. 111 legacy systems and Art. 113 dates as amended. How far can a hotel rely on grandfathering?

### Takeaway
High-risk rules apply from **2 December 2027 (Annex III)** and **2 August 2028 (Annex I)**; 2 August 2026 is the general application date and the Art. 50 date, not a high-risk date. Art. 111(2) as replaced gives private operators of a high-risk system placed on the market or put into service before those dates an open-ended exemption until the system undergoes "significant changes in [its] design", and an Omnibus recital extends this to later units of the same "type and model"; this is the largest real loophole in the Act, but for cloud software that is updated continuously it is fragile, it never covers prohibitions, and the team records that it does not cover Art. 50.

How far a hotel can rely on Art. 111(2) [all my inference]:

| Claim a hotel or vendor might make | Rating | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Our recruitment tool was on the EU market before 2 Dec 2027 and has not changed, so neither the vendor nor we have high-risk duties" | CONDITIONAL, real | This is what Art. 111(2) says. The exemption covers "operators", so it reaches the hotel's Art. 26 deployer duties as well as the vendor's provider duties |
| "We bought it in 2029 but the product type was first sold in 2026" | CONDITIONAL, weaker | Supported only by OMNI recital 39 ("type and model"), which is not operative text, and only "as long as the design ... remains unchanged". The recital also says "lawfully placed" |
| "It is SaaS; the vendor ships updates every few weeks but it is the same product" | FALSE LOOPHOLE in most cases | Recital 128 gives a "change of operating system or software architecture" as an example of substantial modification; retraining, a new model version or new scoring features are changes not foreseen in any conformity assessment (none exists for a legacy system). | "We will rush it into service before 2 Dec 2027 to lock in the exemption" | CONDITIONAL, short-lived | Legal on the text, but freezes the system: no new model, no new purpose. Bias or accuracy fixes may themselves be "significant changes" |
| "It is grandfathered, so we can use it for something new" | FALSE LOOPHOLE | A new intended purpose is a significant change on base recital 177, and a new high-risk purpose triggers Art. 25(1)(c) |
| "Grandfathering covers our chatbot's disclosure duty" | FALSE LOOPHOLE | Art. 111(2) is about high-risk systems; Art. 50 has applied since 2 Aug 2026 (team data: Commission transparency guidelines say so) |
| "Grandfathering covers a banned practice" | FALSE LOOPHOLE | "Without prejudice to the application of Article 5" |
| "Until 2 Dec 2027 nothing applies to our HR tool" | Half true | No AI Act high-risk duty applies yet. Art. 4 literacy, Art. 5, Art. 50 where relevant, GDPR (Arts. 22, 35), and national labour law (Art. 2(11)) apply now |

- **Literal breadth.** Read literally, Art. 111(2) says "this Regulation shall apply to operators of [legacy] high-risk AI systems ... only if" there is a significant change, which would switch off the whole Regulation (bar Art. 5), including Art. 4 and Art. 50, for those operators and systems. The team's reading via the transparency guidelines is narrower (Art. 50 still applies). The narrower reading is the prudent one; the literal one is an unresolved textual point. [my inference]
- **Who judges "significant change"?** No procedure exists: a legacy system has no conformity assessment, no registration and no technical file, so nothing records its "design" as at the cut-off date. A hotel relying on the exemption should keep dated evidence of first placing on the market or putting into service and a vendor change log. [my inference]
- **Asymmetry between private and public operators.** A privately owned hotel has no long-stop date; a hotel run by a public body would have to comply by 2 Aug 2030 if the system is "intended to be used by public authorities". Whether state-owned hotel companies count as "public authorities" is not defined in the Act. [my inference]
- **Provider-switch timing.** Because Art. 25 sits in Chapter III Section 3, a hotel that "becomes a provider" today by using a general chatbot for staff evaluation incurs no Art. 16 obligations until 2 Dec 2027. If that use was "put into service" before the date and is unchanged, Art. 111(2) arguably grandfathers it as well. This is an uncomfortable result of the text and should not be advised as a strategy, since GDPR already bites. [my inference]
- **Commercial reality check.** Enterprise buyers, insurers and works councils will ask for conformity evidence regardless of legal grandfathering; recital 178 encourages voluntary compliance. The exemption is best presented as a buffer for stable legacy tools, not a plan. [my inference]

- No guidance, case or commentary found on what a "significant change in design" means for continuously updated cloud software. Base recital 177 and OMNI recital 39 are the only sources.
- The Commission transparency guidelines (5 Nov 2026) cited in the team data for "Art. 111(2) does not cover Art. 50" were not read by me.
- Whether "lawfully placed" in OMNI recital 39 adds a condition (for example GDPR compliance at the time) is not explained anywhere I could read.

## 5. SME and small mid-cap (SMC) relief after the Omnibus: what is relieved, for whom, and does a hotel qualify?

### Takeaway
There is no SME or SMC exemption from scope: the relief is lighter paperwork for *providers* of high-risk systems, priority sandbox access, guidance, and lower fine ceilings. ### Inferences
- **Does a typical hotel qualify?** [my inference; thresholds are secondary]
  - *Independent hotel, single owner company, no group ties*: almost always an SME (under 250 staff, turnover under EUR 50 m). Gets the lower-of fine cap for everything including Art. 5.
  - *Hotel owned by a property fund, family holding or hotel group*: the SME definition in Rec. 2003/361/EC counts staff and financials of linked enterprises (and a share of partner enterprises). A 60-staff property company owned by a large investor is usually not an SME. This is how the Recommendation works in State-aid and funding practice; I did not re-read it this session.
  - *Regional group with, say, 400 staff and EUR 90 m turnover*: too big for SME, within SMC on the reported thresholds. Gets the lower-of cap for Art. 99(4) and (5) breaches only.
  - *International chain*: neither. Full fines, calculated on the "undertaking".
  - *Franchisee*: assessed on its own figures plus its own linked companies; a franchise contract by itself is not an ownership link. So a franchisee can be an SME while its brand is not. The brand, as provider of a brand-built tool, gets no relief.
- **Reference drafting gap.** Art. 3(14a) points only to "Article 2 of the Annex" to Rec. 2003/361/EC (the thresholds) and Art. 3(14b) only to "point (2) of the Annex" to Rec. 2025/1099. The aggregation rules for partner and linked enterprises sit in other articles of those annexes. Art. 63(1) excludes enterprises with partner or linked enterprises *expressly*, which could be read to mean that elsewhere (Art. 99(6), 11(1), 17(2)) group links are not counted. A regulator is more likely to apply the Recommendation as a whole. Treat "our property company is an SME on stand-alone figures" as CONDITIONAL. [my inference]
- **Relief is a ceiling, not a discount.** Art. 99(6) and (6a) change "whichever is higher" to "whichever is lower"; for a small company 3 % of turnover is far below EUR 15 million, so the practical maximum drops sharply. It does not reduce the duty. [my inference from the text]
- **What a small hotel still owes in full, whatever its size** [my inference from the absence of any size carve-out in the provisions read]: Art. 4 literacy measures; Art. 5 prohibitions; Art. 50 disclosures; from 2 Dec 2027 all Art. 26 deployer duties for any high-risk tool (instructions for use, human oversight, input data, monitoring, logs, informing workers under 26(7) and affected persons under 26(11)).
- **"We are too small for the AI Act" is a FALSE LOOPHOLE.** [my inference]
- **If a small chain becomes a provider** (own-built or re-purposed HR tool), the simplified documentation form (Art. 11), proportionate QMS (Art. 17(2)) and, if it has no partner or linked enterprises, simplified QMS elements (Art. 63) are real but CONDITIONAL help: the Art. 9-15 requirements still apply in full (Art. 63(2)). The simplified form and the Art. 63 guidelines must first be issued by the Commission; I found no evidence that either exists yet.

- Recommendation (EU) 2025/1099 and Recommendation 2003/361/EC could not be opened; thresholds and the linked-enterprise rules are from search summaries and general knowledge.
- No source found on whether the simplified technical documentation form (Art. 11) or the Art. 63 guidelines have been published as of November 2026.

- No source addresses how Art. 3(14a)/(14b) treat partner and linked enterprises.

Team data records that the final transparency guidelines say customer and helpdesk chatbots never qualify as "obvious". - **Human sign-off is not a scope or classification exit** on the draft guidelines. [team data] — [JSON `draftClassificationGuidelines` g1, e2](/home/user/final/tools/ai-act-explorer/ai-act-data.json)

### Inferences
- **Sorting rule for the booklet.** An exit holds when three things are true: it is in an article (not only a recital or guideline), its condition is an observable fact about the system (how it works, what it is used for, when it was first put into service), and the hotel can evidence that fact. An exit fails when it depends on what a party calls the tool, where a server or head office sits, how a contract labels roles, or how small the company is. [my inference]
- **Three exits deserve a plain-language box for hotel readers** because they cover a large share of everyday hotel software: G1 (rules-based automation is not AI), pricing outside Annex III, and G12 (comfort and energy AI in buildings is not a safety component). [my inference]
- **Three false loopholes deserve a warning box** because hotel managers are likely to believe them: "a human always signs off", "it is just a pilot", and "the vendor says it is not high-risk / not AI". [my inference]
- **The biggest unmodelled structural risk is role identity in managed and franchised hotels (G19, G20).** The team's role model has two roles (builder, buyer). Hotel reality has at least four companies around one tool. Until the Commission's Art. 25 guidelines appear, the defensible advice is that each company that either directs the use or uses the output should assume it is a deployer, and that the brand records whether it is provider, distributor, importer or none for every mandated tool. This is also a good interview question area the team already covers under its own guides; no new questions are proposed here. [my inference]
- **Grandfathering (G23) changes procurement timing.** Because the cut-off moved from 2 Aug 2026 to 2 Dec 2027 (and 2 Aug 2028), tools bought and put into service during 2027 can still be legacy systems. Vendors may market this. The honest framing: it is lawful, it lasts only while the product is frozen, and it does nothing for GDPR, Art. 5, Art. 50 or national labour law. [my inference]
- **Literal-text anomalies worth a footnote, not a strategy**: Art. 2(2) and Art. 111(2) both say that only certain articles of "this Regulation" apply to a class of systems, which read literally would also remove Art. 4, 5 or 50 for them. Art. 111(2) expressly preserves Art. 5; Art. 2(2) does not. No source discusses this. [my inference]
