## 1. Who is the "deployer" in owner-operated, managed, franchised and leased hotels, and how do HMAs and franchise agreements split technology, data, cost and liability?

### Takeaway
The AI Act deployer is whoever uses the tool "under its authority" (decides to use it and how), not whoever owns the building or pays the bill. In practice that is the operating company (owner-operated), the management company (managed), the franchisee (franchised) or the tenant (leased), with the brand as a possible co-deployer where its standards impose a tool and its settings. No law-firm source found applies the AI Act roles to HMAs or franchise agreements directly; the contract allocation has to be inferred from how these agreements already handle technology standards, guest data and compliance cost, and those clauses are contested and varied.

### Cited Findings
**Deployer definition and Commission guidance**
- Art. 3(4): a deployer is a natural or legal person "using an AI system under its authority except where the AI system is used in the course of a personal non-professional activity." — [AI Act Service Desk, Art. 3](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-3); [Recital 13](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/recital-13)
- Commission material states that where the deployer is a legal person, the individual employees acting under its instructions and control are not themselves deployers. — [Commission digital-strategy page, via search summary](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/node/17084)
- The Commission's Art. 50 transparency guidelines (C(2026) 7702 final, 5 Nov 2026) say technical control of the system is not needed to be a deployer, and that this holds inside group structures. — D7 booklet, section A4, citing [European Commission, C(2026) 7702](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/) (team's reading of the guideline; re-check exact wording in the guideline before quoting)
- D7 booklet table "Who is responsible in your hotel" (team's reading, not a Commission table): Owner-operated → operating company; Managed → management company, plus the brand where its standards impose a tool and how it is used, owner usually not unless it decides on the tools; Franchised → franchisee, plus brand where standards impose tool and settings; Leased → tenant operating the hotel. Duties towards staff (consultation, data protection) fall on the employer, which may differ from the AI Act deployer. — D7 booklet, section A4
- A group chatbot built on a large language model makes the group the provider ("maker") of that system; branding, modifying or repurposing a tool are the other routes to provider status (Art. 25). — D7 booklet, section B (WATCH FOR), citing the AI Act
- Fines are a share of the "undertaking's" worldwide turnover; under the GDPR the same word follows competition law and can cover a whole group; whether AI Act authorities read it the same way is untested. — D7 booklet, section A4, citing [GDPR](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- AI Office direct enforcement (new Arts. 75a–75d) does not reach hotels; hotels stay with national market surveillance authorities. — team's `docs/d7-governance/OMNIBUS-REVISION-LIST.md`, citing [Reg. (EU) 2026/1744](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)
- Hotel-sector legal commentary: a hotel that develops or commissions a custom-built AI solution is a provider; a hotel using ready-made software almost always counts as a deployer. — [Thomas Nietsch interview, DialogShift (vendor blog)](https://www.dialogshift.com/en/blog-posts/rechtsanwalt-thomas-nietsch-ai-act-in-der-hotellerie)

**How HMAs and franchise agreements allocate technology, data and risk**
- Under an HMA the operator typically acts as the owner's agent; under a franchise the brand supplies trademarks and system specifications while operations stay with the franchisee/operator; where a white-label manager runs a franchised hotel, the owner should read the HMA and franchise agreement together to see which services sit under each. — [Fieldfisher, "Hotel franchise or management agreement?" (search summary)](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/insights/hotel-franchise-or-management-agreement); [Fieldfisher, "HMAs: 10 key points for hotel owners"](https://www.fieldfisher.com/en/services/franchising/franchise-commercial-law-blog/hotel-management-agreements-10-key-points-for-hotel-owners)
- Guest data under HMAs: traditionally available to both owner and operator during and after the term; more recently some operators stipulate guest data is the operator's "property", limiting owner access to the term; most HMAs restrict owner use of guest data to operating the hotel. — [Conventus Law, HMA negotiations (2017)](https://conventuslaw.com/report/hotel-management-agreement-negotiations-the-issues/)
- Sample clauses range from joint ownership of "Hotel Guest Information" with a copy to the owner on termination, to franchisor ownership of customer data. — [Law Insider, Hotel Guest Data clauses](https://lawinsider.com/clause/hotel-guest-data/_1); [Embassy Suites FDD excerpt, "information obtained by franchisee becomes property"](https://www.fdd.ninja/brands/embassy_suites/2025/information_obtained_franchisee_becomes_property)
- Owner-side advisers recommend owners secure ownership of guest and operational data plus approval rights over any PMS change. — [Horwath HTL, "Your hotel's data: who actually controls and owns it?"](https://horwathhtl.com/insight/your-hotels-data-who-actually-controls-and-owns-it/)
- GDPR roles are contested: CMS reports that some hotel operators take the line that owner–manager and franchisor–franchisee relationships create no controller–processor or joint-controller relationship, while others accept one is likely and amend contracts; CMS calls the first position surprising. — [CMS, "Hotels and GDPR"](https://cms.law/content/download/333455/file/Hotels%20GDPR.pdf)
- Franchise networks: roles are often insufficiently specified in the data-protection appendix; controller status is a factual question that depends on negotiating power and franchisee independence. — [Lawrope (search summary)](https://lawrope.com/?p=4227)
- Joint controllers must have a written arrangement allocating responsibilities (GDPR Art. 26). — [GDPR, Art. 26](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- D7 booklet recommendation: management and franchise agreements should state who bears the cost of compliance and of fines; owners carry the money risk because they approve the spend and sign the agreements. — D7 booklet, sections A4 and B1

### Inferences
- *(our reading)* A register therefore cannot have one fixed "deployer" field per hotel. It needs a per-tool role field (provider / deployer / co-deployer / importer-distributor / none) and a separate "employer of affected staff" field, because the AI Act deployer, the GDPR controller and the labour-law employer can be three different companies in a managed or franchised hotel.
- *(our reading)* Owners in managed hotels are rarely deployers but are the ones who need the register most for asset value (M&A, re-branding, operator change). The register should be portable: the owner should have a contractual right to a copy at termination, mirroring the guest-data clauses above.
- *(our reading)* The brand is most likely to be a co-deployer for mandated guest-facing tools (brand chatbot, loyalty personalisation, brand-set pricing engine) and least likely for property-chosen tools (a kitchen waste camera bought by the F&B director).

### Gaps
- No law-firm briefing was found (or could be fetched) that writes AI Act roles or AI clauses into HMAs or franchise agreements specifically (searched HSF, Hogan Lovells, Baker McKenzie, Bird & Bird, Taylor Wessing, Withers). The Bird & Bird piece "Deploying AI in a hotel chain" exists ([link](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2024/global/deploying-ai-in-a-hotel-chain-how-to-mitigate-privacy-and-digital-regulatory-risk)) but could not be opened; it predates the Omnibus.
- No AI Act Service Desk FAQ was found on whether one group entity can use a system "under the authority" of another group entity.
- No source found on typical indemnity language for AI or regulatory fines in HMAs/franchise agreements.
- Leased hotels: no source found specific to AI or data allocation between landlord and tenant-operator.

## 2. How do groups manage technology across properties today, and how are GDPR records and DPIAs kept at group vs property level?

### Takeaway
Brands control the core stack through approved-vendor lists and technical standards (PMS, CRS, payment gateway, loyalty), with varying strictness and waiver routes. That gives the brand a natural place to hold the group register for mandated systems, while property-chosen tools (F&B, spa, local marketing) sit outside it unless each hotel reports them. No published source describes how hotel groups actually split GDPR Art. 30 records and DPIAs between group and property.

### Cited Findings
- IHG approved Oracle OPERA Cloud (Jan 2026) as an approved PMS for its Americas and EMEAA estates; franchisees and owners "can select OPERA Cloud while meeting IHG standards". — [Hospitality Net / Oracle release](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4130667.html); [IT Brief UK](https://itbrief.co.uk/story/ihg-approves-oracle-opera-cloud-for-hotels-globally)
- Franchise disclosure documents (US FDDs, illustrative of the brand model): Sonesta requires franchisees to adopt a PMS from an approved list; Crowne Plaza's franchisor designates approved vendors for property-level technology but allows waiver requests; Hawthorn Suites by Wyndham lets franchisees buy PMS hardware from any source meeting brand standards but requires card tokenization through an approved gateway. — [Sonesta FDD 2025](https://www.fdd.ninja/brands/sonesta_select_sonesta_essential/2025/systems_franchisees_required_use_besides_pms); [Crowne Plaza FDD 2025](https://www.fdd.ninja/brands/crowne_plaza/2025/designates_acceptable_thirdparty_vendors_propertylevel_technology); [Hawthorn Suites FDD 2025](https://www.fdd.ninja/brands/hawthorn_suites_by_wyndham/2025/requirements_purchasing_hardware_pms)
- Trade commentary: Hilton takes full control of its tech stack (legacy OnQ, migrating to HotelKey cloud); Marriott runs its own PMS and an Amadeus CRS and sits between the extremes. Source is a 2024 overview and may be dated. — [Hospitality Investor, "2024 hotel tech vendor overview"](https://www.hospitalityinvestor.com/technology/2024-hotel-tech-vendor-overview)
- Oracle's OPERA Cloud Assistant (16 Jun 2026) brought AI features into the PMS at no extra cost for existing customers: staff Q&A assistant, AI-written rate-code descriptions, room-assignment recommendations; Wyndham is launch partner with 2,100+ properties on OPERA Cloud. — [Oracle press release, 16 Jun 2026](https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/new-ai-capabilities-in-oracle-opera-cloud-supercharge-hotel-operations-2026-06-16/); [Skift, 16 Jun 2026](https://skift.com/2026/06/16/oracle-opera-cloud-ai-assistant-rooms-rates-2026/)
- PMS vendors are normally processors and the hotel the controller; the hotel remains accountable to guests for a vendor breach. — [Schellman, GDPR FAQ for hospitality](https://www.schellman.com/hubfs/whitepapers/gdpr-faq-for-hospitality.pdf) (general guidance)
- Marriott/Starwood breach shows group-level exposure from acquired systems: the ICO fined Marriott International for the breach of the Starwood reservation database it inherited. — [ICO penalty notice, Marriott (30 Oct 2020)](https://ico.org.uk/action-weve-taken/enforcement/marriott-international-inc/); [CMS, "Marriott data breach and GDPR"](https://cms.law/en/int/legal-updates/Marriott-data-breach-and-GDPR)
- GDPR Art. 30 requires each controller and each processor to keep its own record of processing activities; Art. 35 requires a DPIA for high-risk processing, and a single DPIA may cover a set of similar processing operations. — [GDPR Arts. 30, 35(1)](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- The Omnibus amended AI Act Art. 27(4)–(5) so a fundamental rights impact assessment can cross-refer to or incorporate parts of a GDPR DPIA; most hotels owe no FRIA, except deployers of Annex III 5(b) systems such as guest credit scoring. — team's `OMNIBUS-REVISION-LIST.md`, citing [Reg. (EU) 2026/1744](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)
- D7 booklet recommends: keep a group register of AI tools fed by each hotel's list; publish a short list of approved AI tools with work accounts; run AI literacy across the group with an internal record. — D7 booklet, section B (brand/CIO), citing [Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe KI und Datenschutz (6 May 2024)](https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/20240506_DSK_Orientierungshilfe_KI_und_Datenschutz.pdf) and [Commission AI literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers)
- Adoption context for chains vs independents: h2c study (2026) finds AI adoption widespread among chains but enterprise readiness limited. — [Hospitality Net, h2c study](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/news/4134666/new-h2c-study-ai-adoption-is-widespread-among-hotel-chains-but-enterprise-readiness-remains-limited) (headline only; sample not verified)

### Inferences
- *(our reading)* The GDPR Art. 30 record is the closest existing analogue and the natural template: one record per controller, with the brand/management company keeping central records for central systems and each owning/operating entity keeping its own. An AI register that links each AI tool to its Art. 30 entry and DPIA avoids a parallel bureaucracy.
- *(our reading)* A group register works in two tiers: a central "approved AI catalogue" maintained by brand IT (with vendor due diligence done once), and a property-level "in use here" list that records which catalogue tools are switched on, local settings, local owner and local staff consultation. Property-chosen tools outside the catalogue go into the same property list flagged "local".
- *(our reading)* Because brands use approved-vendor lists with waivers, the waiver process is the obvious trigger for a register entry for non-standard tools.

### Gaps
- No source found describing how a named European hotel group actually keeps Art. 30 records or DPIAs (central vs per property). Annual reports of Accor, IHG, Whitbread, Meliá, NH/Minor were not reached in this session.
- No source found for Accor, Meliá or NH approved-vendor rules in Europe; FDD evidence is US-only.

## 3. Which AI tools sit in in-hotel F&B, and which laws touch them?

### Takeaway
In-hotel F&B AI is mostly minimal-risk under the AI Act, with Art. 50 transparency for ordering bots, booking voice agents and talking service robots. The heavier obligations come from other laws: labour law for kitchen cameras (German works councils co-decide on any device able to monitor staff), PCI DSS when card data passes through a bot, food information law for allergen answers (the hotel stays responsible whatever tool produced the answer), and product rules (Product Liability Directive, Cyber Resilience Act) for smart kitchen equipment and anything the group builds itself.

### Cited Findings
**Tools**
- Winnow: camera plus scale over the bin; camera captures a burst of 30+ images per disposal; vendor claims 3,000+ commercial kitchen deployments and hotel customers including Marriott and IHG; vendor claims waste halved and 2–8% food-cost savings within 12 months (vendor figures, not independently verified). — [Hospitality Net, "Winnow brings AI into the commercial kitchen"](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4092645.html); [Hotel Dive](https://www.hoteldive.com/news/ai-assisted-kitchen-solution-to-reduce-food-waste/649553/); [CB Insights, Winnow customers](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/winnow/customers)
- Orbisk (NL): above-bin camera identifying food before it enters the bin; vendor claims 800+ ingredients at ~90% accuracy and hotel-group customers including Hyatt, Accor and Marriott (self-reported). — [Orbisk](https://orbisk.com/?p=25548439)
- Leanpath (founded 2004): connected scale and camera, manual or touchless AI. — [Bloomberg, "AI-enhanced garbage bins" (1 Jul 2024)](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-07-01/ai-enhanced-garbage-bins-aim-to-solve-food-service-s-waste-problem)
- Rational iCombi Pro: "iCookingSuite" uses sensors and algorithms to adjust temperature, climate, air speed and time; ConnectedCooking (Wi-Fi) handles software updates, recipe transfer across units and HACCP documentation. Rational does not call it "AI". — [Rational, full-service hotels](https://rational-online.com/en_xx/industry/full-service-hotels-c); [Rational iCombi brochure](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/1204/5544/files/Rational_Combi_Brochure.pdf)
- Restaurant voice/phone agents take and change bookings, take orders and answer menu, dietary and allergen questions; European examples include Zenchef (France, Belgium) and Fonio; vendor materials only, no independent accuracy evidence found. — [Hackceleration review (vendor-comparison blog)](https://hackceleration.com/labs/best/ai-voice-agent-restaurants); [AINORA guide (vendor)](https://ainora.lt/blog/ai-voice-assistant-restaurants-guide)
- D7 booklet F&B classification: ordering bots and table-booking phone agents → transparency; service robots that talk to guests → transparency if not obviously a machine; food-waste cameras → minimal, but high-risk if footage or logs are used to rate individual cooks (draft high-risk guidelines, 19 May 2026); smart ovens/cleaning robots → minimal for the hotel, ask the maker, AI controlling gas appliances can be a safety component from 2 Aug 2028; menu pricing, covers and production forecasting, recipe tools → minimal. — D7 booklet, section C4, citing the AI Act and Commission draft Art. 6 guidelines

**Laws**
- AI Act Art. 50: people must be told they are interacting with an AI system unless obvious; applies from 2 Aug 2026. — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50, Art. 113](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- AI Act Art. 26(7): before putting a high-risk AI system into service at the workplace, employer-deployers must inform workers' representatives and affected workers (relevant only if a kitchen camera is used to rate individual cooks). — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 26(7)](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- Germany, §87(1) no. 6 BetrVG: works council co-determines the introduction and use of technical devices "suitable" to monitor employee behaviour or performance; intent to monitor is not required; Taylor Wessing (24 Jun 2026) says this opens co-determination in many AI scenarios; counter-example: ArbG Hamburg (24 BVGa 1/24) found no co-determination for staff ChatGPT use on personal accounts because the employer could not collect behaviour or performance data through it. — [Taylor Wessing, "KI als Arbeitsmittel" (24 Jun 2026)](https://www.taylorwessing.com/de/insights-and-events/insights/2026/06/ki-als-arbeitsmittel-warum-die-mitbestimmung-frueh-mitdenken); [activeMind, ArbG Hamburg ruling](https://www.activemind.legal/de/guides/urteil-betriebsrat-ki/); [CMS, "Einführung von KI – Einbindung des Betriebsrats"](https://cms.law/de/deu/legal-updates/einfuehrung-von-ki-im-unternehmen-einbindung-des-betriebsrats)
- Group-level works council (Konzernbetriebsrat) can be competent for camera systems rolled out across a group. — [Rechtslupe, "Überwachungskameras – Zuständigkeit des Konzernbetriebsrats"](https://www.rechtslupe.de/arbeitsrecht/ueberwachungskameras-und-die-zustaendigkeit-des-konzernbetriebsrat-3107929)
- France: the CSE (firms of 50+) must be consulted before introducing new technologies (Code du travail L2312-8); a Paris appeal court ruling of 21 May 2026 held that approving a chatbot staff already use, or writing a charter for it, counts as introducing it. — D7 booklet, citing [Code du travail art. L2312-8](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/codes/article_lc/LEGIARTI000043884445) and Cour d'appel de Paris, 21 May 2026
- Spain: employers using algorithms on staff must inform workers' representatives (Estatuto de los Trabajadores art. 64.4(d)). — D7 booklet, citing [ET art. 64.4(d)](https://www.boe.es/eli/es/rdlg/2015/10/23/2/con)
- Switzerland: Ordinance 3 to the Labour Act, Art. 26, bans systems intended to monitor staff behaviour. — D7 booklet, citing [ArGV 3 Art. 26 (SR 822.113)](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1993/2564_2564_2564/en)
- PCI DSS: card numbers spoken to a phone agent or typed into a chatbot bring the tool into PCI scope; PCI SSC AI guidance (Sept 2026, non-binding) recommends tokens instead of card numbers and human oversight of AI agents. — D7 booklet, citing PCI DSS v4.0.1 and [PCI SSC, "Security considerations for AI systems" v1.0 (Sept 2026)](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/)
- Food information: Regulation (EU) 1169/2011 makes the food business operator under whose name the food is marketed responsible for food information (Art. 8) and requires allergen information also for non-prepacked food, e.g. restaurant meals (Arts. 9(1)(c), 44). — [Regulation (EU) 1169/2011](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj)
- Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 covers software including AI, applies to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 Dec 2026; a group that builds its own menu/allergen assistant and puts it into use could answer as manufacturer; courts can order disclosure and presume defect. — [Directive (EU) 2024/2853](http://data.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/2853/oj); D7 booklet, section C4 (our reading on the allergen case)
- Cyber Resilience Act: main obligations for connected products apply from 11 Dec 2027 (vulnerability-reporting duties from 11 Sep 2026) — relevant to connected ovens, robots and kiosks the hotel buys. — [Regulation (EU) 2024/2847, Art. 71](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/2847/oj)
- Food-waste cameras fit EU food-waste policy; one vendor page claims EU mandates require large food-service operators to measure waste — not independently confirmed in this session. — [Orbisk (vendor claim)](https://orbisk.com/?p=25548982)

### Inferences
- *(our reading)* For F&B the register's most useful fields are not AI Act tier (almost always minimal/transparency) but: "can it see or measure staff?" (labour trigger), "does it touch card data?" (PCI), "does it give allergen or nutrition answers?" (1169/2011 accountability, human check), "is it a connected product?" (CRA proof from 11 Dec 2027), and "did we build or rebrand it?" (provider/manufacturer).
- *(our reading)* Kitchen tools are often bought by the executive chef or F&B director, or come inside equipment (Rational), so they are the most likely category to be missing from a group register. Equipment with firmware updates over ConnectedCooking-type links can gain new features without a procurement event.
- *(our reading)* Banqueting/MICE and room-service ordering bots typically sit on the brand's or PMS vendor's stack, while restaurant booking tools are often chosen locally; the register should record F&B outlet (restaurant, bar, room service, banqueting, kitchen, staff canteen) so outlet managers can see their own list.

### Gaps
- No independent (non-vendor) evidence found on accuracy of AI allergen answers or on adoption rates of waste cameras among European hotels.
- No source found on service robots in European hotel F&B (e.g. Pudu/Bear Robotics deployments) or on AI inventory/procurement tools (e.g. Apicbase, Choco) — not searched within the budget.
- No ruling found specifically on kitchen waste cameras and works councils; the application of §87(1) no. 6 BetrVG to them is inferred from the general rule.
- UK allergen rules (Natasha's Law for prepacked-for-direct-sale food) were not researched.

## 4. What day-to-day workflows would a register support?

### Takeaway
The register's job is to make existing duties traceable: Art. 4 AI literacy records, Art. 50 notices, Art. 26 deployer duties for any high-risk tool (from 2 Dec 2027), GDPR Art. 30 records and DPIAs, staff-representative consultation, vendor due diligence, and contract renewal before 2 Dec 2027. Most of these already have a home in hotel groups (procurement, DPO, HR); the register is the index that links them per tool and per property.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 4 (as amended by the Omnibus) keeps an AI-literacy duty for providers and deployers but states it does not require guaranteeing any specific level of literacy for any individual. — team's `OMNIBUS-REVISION-LIST.md`, citing [Reg. (EU) 2026/1744](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)
- Commission AI literacy Q&A (20 Oct 2026) sets out a four-step approach; the D7 booklet recommends keeping an internal record. — D7 booklet, citing [Commission AI literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers)
- Deployers of high-risk systems must use them per instructions, assign human oversight to competent people, monitor operation, keep automatically generated logs for at least six months, and inform workers' representatives before workplace use (Art. 26(1), (2), (5), (6), (7)); these apply from 2 Dec 2027 for Annex III systems. — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 26](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [Reg. (EU) 2026/1744](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj)
- D7 booklet's vendor due diligence: seven vendor questions plus logs kept at least six months, data stored in the EU or a stated transfer basis, and Cyber Resilience Act conformity for connected products from 11 Dec 2027. — D7 booklet, sections A3 and B
- D7 booklet's to-do for owners/asset managers: check management and franchise agreements for who is the user of each tool and who is the maker of anything built. — D7 booklet, section B1
- Mews survey (500+ properties): properties with a formal AI policy report 92% trust in AI vs 49% without one. — [Hotel Management, "Mews: most hoteliers use AI daily"](https://www.hotelmanagement.net/tech/mews-most-hoteliers-use-ai-daily-human-touch-still-matters) (survey method not verified; vendor survey)
- State of Hotel AI (Hospitality Daily / Destination AI, 2026; reported sample 107 hotel company leaders, led by management companies 33% and owners/investors 28%): 63% use AI regularly, 12.6% have a scaled strategy, 35% do not measure ROI, under 6% record AI savings in the P&L. — [Hospitality Net, Destination AI Summit recap](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4134731/not-done-weekly-destination-ai-summit-recap) (secondary recap; sample size from recap only)

### Inferences
*(our reading; scenarios built from the duties above, not from a published hotel practice)*
- **Onboarding a new tool:** property or department requests → check approved catalogue → if new, vendor questionnaire, AI Act tier, provider/deployer role, GDPR role + Art. 30 link, DPIA screen, staff-rep screen, PCI screen → approve → entry created with owner, outlet, go-live date.
- **Works-council / CSE consultation:** register flags "can monitor staff" → consultation record and agreement date stored; blocks go-live in DE/FR/ES until filled.
- **AI literacy (Art. 4):** each entry lists user roles; briefing records attach per role and property, so a GM can show who was briefed.
- **Incident handling:** wrong allergen answer, bot discloses guest data, pricing error → incident logged against tool → links to vendor contract, DPO, (for high-risk from 2 Dec 2027) Art. 26(5) duty to inform provider/authority.
- **Owner reporting:** management company sends owner a periodic extract (tools in use, cost, open risks, contract end dates) — fits the existing owner-reporting rhythm of HMAs.
- **Audits / brand QA:** brand compares property list against catalogue; finds local tools and switched-on bundled features.
- **M&A / operator change / re-flagging:** register extract is part of due diligence; shows which tools and data go with the asset, which leave with the brand, and which contracts need novation.
- **Contract renewal before 2 Dec 2027:** contract end dates and an "Annex III exposure" flag let procurement put AI Act clauses (instructions for use, logs, CE marking where high-risk, cooperation) into renewals now.

## 5. Pain points and edge cases: shadow AI, bundled AI, vendor-pushed features, multi-country groups

### Takeaway
The largest blind spots are AI the hotel never "bought": general-purpose chatbots staff use on their own, and AI features vendors switch on inside existing PMS/CRM/equipment contracts. Multi-country groups face one AI Act for EU companies but different labour-consultation rules per country, and no AI Act in Switzerland or the UK.

### Cited Findings
- Shadow AI: opinion piece calls it "today's reality in most hotels", argues against blanket bans because staff then hide use. — [Hospitality Net, "Your staff are already using AI — you don't know about it"](https://www.hospitalitynet.org/opinion/4132350/your-staff-are-already-using-ai-you-dont-know-about-it) (opinion; no survey data)
- A Fall 2026 hotel survey reportedly found 72% of respondents access AI through general-purpose tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot, and that experimentation drives adoption more than top-down mandates. — [PhocusWire, "Hotel adoption of general-purpose AI tools outpaces specialized solutions"](https://www.phocuswire.com/news/technology/hotel-adoption-of-general-purpose-ai-tools-outpaces-specialized-solutions) (figure from search summary; survey identity and sample not confirmed — check before use)
- German data protection authorities recommend work accounts over private accounts for AI tools. — D7 booklet, citing [Datenschutzkonferenz, Orientierungshilfe KI (6 May 2024)](https://www.datenschutzkonferenz-online.de/media/oh/20240506_DSK_Orientierungshilfe_KI_und_Datenschutz.pdf)
- ArbG Hamburg (24 BVGa 1/24): no works-council co-determination for staff ChatGPT use via personal accounts, since the employer collects no behaviour data. — [activeMind](https://www.activemind.legal/de/guides/urteil-betriebsrat-ki/)
- France: per a Paris appeal court ruling (21 May 2026), approving a chatbot staff already use, or writing a charter for it, counts as introducing it and requires prior CSE consultation. — D7 booklet, citing Cour d'appel de Paris, 21 May 2026
- Bundled AI: Oracle added AI to OPERA Cloud for all customers at no extra cost (June 2026), across front desk, revenue management and configuration. — [Oracle press release](https://www.oracle.com/news/announcement/new-ai-capabilities-in-oracle-opera-cloud-supercharge-hotel-operations-2026-06-16/); [Altexsoft](https://www.altexsoft.com/travel-industry-news/oracle-puts-ai-in-opera-cloud-to-cut-front-desk-friction/)
- Agents and assistants linked to the PMS and CRM are classified on what the whole system does (draft Commission high-risk guidelines). — D7 booklet, citing Commission draft Art. 6 guidelines (19 May 2026)
- Repurposing or substantially modifying a system can turn a deployer into a provider (Art. 25). — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 25](http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- Multi-country: Switzerland has no AI act; the FADP applies to AI directly and the FDPIC says people must be told when they talk to a machine (contested); the UK has no AI act and allows machine-only decisions with safeguards under the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025; the EU AI Act still applies to the group's EU companies. — D7 booklet, citing [FDPIC (8 May 2025)](https://edoeb.admin.ch/en/update-current-legislation-directly-applicable-ai) and [UK GDPR as amended](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679)
- Labour consultation differs per country: DE works council co-determination, FR CSE consultation (50+ staff), ES individual algorithm information duty, CH ban on behaviour-monitoring systems. — D7 booklet, section "Tags", citing BetrVG, Code du travail L2312-8, ET art. 64.4(d), ArGV 3 Art. 26
- Industry lobbying context: HOTREC and other employer groups (Dec 2025) urged the European Parliament to drop the legislative annex of its report on AI and algorithmic management at work, favouring a non-legislative approach. — [Journal des Palaces, press release](https://www.journaldespalaces.com/en/pressrelease-76860-calling-for-a-balanced-and-non-legislative-approach-on-ai-at-work.html)
- A Romanian article attributes to a "HOTREC European Hotel Distribution Study 2026" the figure that about 26% of European hotels use AI solutions. — [Revista Biz (secondary)](https://www.revistabiz.ro/inteligenta-artificiala-si-digitalizarea-redeseneaza-viitorul-ospitalitatii/) (not verified against HOTREC; sample unknown — do not use without the original study)
- Pricing tools: competitors using the same pricing algorithm or feeding sensitive price data into a shared tool can amount to price fixing (Art. 101 TFEU; Eturas C-74/14). — D7 booklet, citing [Commission horizontal guidelines 2023/C 259/01](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52023XC0721(01)) and Eturas

### Inferences
- *(our reading)* A register needs a "how it arrived" field: bought / bundled in suite / switched on by vendor update / built in-house / personal tool used for work. The last three are the ones procurement never sees.
- *(our reading)* Vendor contracts should require notice before AI features are enabled, so a release note becomes a register trigger. PMS release notes (e.g. OPERA Cloud) are a practical monthly check for brand IT.
- *(our reading)* A sanctioned shortlist with work accounts (per DSK guidance) turns shadow AI into register entries; in France that approval itself triggers CSE consultation, so the register should hold the consultation step before approval.
- *(our reading)* For multi-country groups, the register should store the applicable regime per entry (EU AI Act / CH FADP / UK GDPR) and the labour step per country, with one shared tool record and per-country property rows. Swiss and UK hotels of an EU group are outside the AI Act unless output is used in the EU (Art. 2(1)(c)); the register still helps group consistency.
- *(our reading)* Edge cases worth a register rule: outsourced F&B (a tenant restaurant inside the hotel is its own deployer and employer; the hotel records it only as a third-party outlet); white-label manager + franchise (two contracts, two possible deployers); staff employed by an agency (employer for consultation is the agency).

### Gaps
- No hotel-specific survey quantifying shadow AI (unsanctioned tool use) was found.
- No source on Italian or UK specific consultation rules for AI at work was gathered in this session (Italy's AI law and Statuto dei lavoratori Art. 4 not researched).
- No evidence found on how vendors notify hotels of new AI features in updates, or contract clauses that control this.
- Tenant-run restaurants inside hotels (leased F&B outlets): no source found on AI or data allocation between hotel and tenant.
